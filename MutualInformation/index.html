Mutual information

How much does knowing one variable tell you about another? Drag the circles.

Each circle is the uncertainty in one random variable. Where they overlap is what the two share. Six lessons follow, and each one has an explanation, a lab you can poke at, and a short quiz.

Warm-up: entropy in one minute

Everything in this guide is built from one quantity. The surprise of an outcome with probability $p$ is $-\log_2 p$ bits. A rare outcome is very surprising, and a certain outcome is not surprising at all. The entropy of a random variable is its average surprise:

$H(X)=-\sum_{x}p(x)\log_2 p(x)=\mathbb{E}[-\log_2 p(X)]$

Three relatives of entropy will keep coming up. The joint entropy $H(X,Y)$ is the entropy of the pair. The conditional entropy $H(Y|X)=\sum_x p(x)H(Y|X=x)$ is the uncertainty left in $Y$ after you learn $X$. The two are tied together by the chain rule, $H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y|X)$. The last relative is the Kullback–Leibler divergence $D_{\text{KL}}(p\Vert q)=\sum_x p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$, which is never negative and is zero only when $p=q$.

Conventions: $0\log 0=0$. Logs are base 2, so everything is measured in bits. Natural logs give nats, and 1 bit = $\ln 2\approx 0.693$ nats.

1The definition

Take two random variables $X$ and $Y$ with joint distribution $p(x,y)$ and marginals $p(x)$ and $p(y)$. If they were independent, the joint would factor as $p(x)p(y)$. Mutual information measures how far the true joint is from that factorized version.

$I(X;Y)=\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\,\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}$

The same quantity can be written in three other ways, and you will want all of them:

$I(X;Y)=D_{\text{KL}}(p(x,y)\Vert p(x)p(y))$
$I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)=H(Y)-H(Y|X)$
$I(X;Y)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)$
Derivation: why the entropy form equals the definition

Rewrite the ratio inside the log as a conditional probability, $\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}=\frac{p(x|y)}{p(x)}$, and split the log:

$I(X;Y)=\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x|y)}{p(x)}=-\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log p(x)+\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log p(x|y)$

In the first sum, $\sum_y p(x,y)=p(x)$, so it equals $H(X)$. The second sum is $-H(X|Y)$. Therefore $I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)$. Swapping the roles of $X$ and $Y$ gives the other form, and combining with the chain rule $H(X|Y)=H(X,Y)-H(Y)$ gives the inclusion–exclusion form.

For continuous variables, replace sums with integrals. MI is still well defined and non-negative, even though differential entropy can be negative:

$I(X;Y)=\int\int p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}\,dx\,dy$

Properties worth memorizing

  • Symmetric. $I(X;Y)=I(Y;X)$. This is obvious from the formula, and it is not true of $H(X|Y)$.
  • Non-negative. $I(X;Y)\ge 0$, because it is a KL divergence (Gibbs' inequality, which follows from Jensen).
  • Zero exactly when independent. $I(X;Y)=0 \iff p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)$.
  • Bounded by the entropies. $I(X;Y)\le\min\{H(X),H(Y)\}$ for discrete variables. In the extreme, $I(X;X)=H(X)$, so entropy is a variable's “self-information”.
  • Blind to labels. Relabelling the values of $X$ (any invertible map) leaves $I$ unchanged. Only the pattern of co-occurrence matters, not the numeric values. Section 5 builds on this.
  • Data processing. Processing can only destroy information: $I(X;g(Y))\le I(X;Y)$ for any function $g$.

2Interpretation

MI is one number with several equivalent readings. Each reading is the right mental model in a different situation.

$I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)$

Before you see $Y$, your uncertainty about $X$ is $H(X)$ bits. After you see it, the average uncertainty left is $H(X|Y)$. Mutual information is the average drop. If you were playing twenty questions to identify $X$, knowing $Y$ would save you $I(X;Y)$ yes/no questions on average.

The word “average” matters. A particular unlucky value of $y$ can leave you more uncertain than before. But averaged over $y$, information never hurts: $I\ge 0$.

$I(X;Y)=D_{\text{KL}}(p(x,y)\Vert p(x)p(y))$

The product $p(x)p(y)$ is the best model of the pair that assumes no relationship. MI is the KL divergence from the truth to that model.

In coding terms, an ideal code built for the independent model spends $-\log p(x)p(y)$ bits on the pair $(x,y)$. The ideal code for the true joint spends $-\log p(x,y)$ bits. The average waste is exactly $I(X;Y)$ bits per pair. Dependence means there is redundancy you would miss by treating the variables separately.

$\mathrm{pmi}(x;y)=\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}=\log\frac{p(x|y)}{p(x)}$

Pointwise mutual information is the log of the factor by which observing $y$ rescales the probability of $x$. A value of $+1$ bit means $y$ doubles the probability of $x$. A value of $-1$ bit means $y$ halves it. MI is the expectation of this quantity:

$I(X;Y)=\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}[\mathrm{pmi}(X;Y)]$

Individual pmi values can be negative, but their average cannot. This is the number the table in the lab below shows for every cell.

A noisy channel $p(y|x)$ turns inputs into outputs. For a given input distribution, $I(X;Y)$ is the number of bits per use that actually get through. The best you can do over all input distributions is Shannon's channel capacity:

$C=\max_{p(x)}I(X;Y)$

For the binary symmetric channel that flips each bit with probability $\varepsilon$, the capacity is $C=1-H_b(\varepsilon)$, reached with a uniform input. You can verify this in the lab below.

Where you will meet it in machine learning

  • Decision trees and feature selection. Information gain is $I(\text{feature};\text{label})$.
  • Representation learning. InfoMax and contrastive methods such as InfoNCE maximize lower bounds on $I$ between views or layers.
  • Information bottleneck. Compress $X$ into $T$ while keeping $I(T;Y)$ high: $\min I(X;T)-\beta\,I(T;Y)$.
  • Clustering evaluation. Normalized MI compares two partitions of the same data.

3A worked example: does this word predict spam?

You have 1000 emails, of which 300 are spam. For one candidate word, you recorded whether each email contains it. Let $S\in\{\text{spam},\text{ham}\}$ be the label and $W\in\{\text{has word},\text{no word}\}$ be the indicator. How much does the word tell you about the label?

This is how MI-based feature selection ranks features, and it is the same quantity a decision tree computes as “information gain” when it chooses a split. The lab walks through the computation one step at a time. You can edit the counts, and everything recomputes.

What the ranking teaches

  • “the” appears in nearly every email at the same rate in both classes, so its MI is about zero.
  • “winner” is a perfect indicator of spam whenever it appears, but it appears in only 4.5% of emails. MI is an average over all emails, so a feature that is informative only rarely scores low.
  • “free” is imperfect but frequent. It wins because it carries some information about many emails.
  • Because MI is symmetric, the same number also answers the reverse question: how much does the label tell you about the word?
  • With small samples these estimates are noisy and biased upward. Section 5 shows how large the bias is.

4Conditional mutual information

The dependence between $X$ and $Y$ can change when you learn a third variable $Z$. Conditional mutual information measures the dependence that remains once $Z$ is known. It is the MI computed inside each slice $Z=z$, averaged over $z$.

$I(X;Y|Z)=\sum_z p(z)\,I(X;Y|Z{=}z)=\sum_{x,y,z}p(x,y,z)\log\frac{p(x,y|z)}{p(x|z)\,p(y|z)}$

It has the same family of equivalent forms as before, with everything conditioned on $Z$:

$I(X;Y|Z)=H(X|Z)-H(X|Y,Z)=H(X,Z)+H(Y,Z)-H(Z)-H(X,Y,Z)$

It is zero exactly when $X$ and $Y$ are conditionally independent given $Z$, written $X\indep Y\mid Z$. When $X\to Z\to Y$ is a Markov chain, this holds.

The chain rule

$I(X;Y,Z)=I(X;Z)+I(X;Y|Z)$

The information that $(Y,Z)$ carries about $X$ equals what $Z$ tells you, plus what $Y$ adds beyond $Z$. In general, $I(X;Y_1,\dots,Y_n)=\sum_i I(X;Y_i|Y_1,\dots,Y_{i-1})$. This is the bookkeeping rule behind many information-theoretic proofs, including the one in Section 6.

Conditioning can go either way

It is tempting to think that knowing more should reduce dependence. It does not always.

  • Dependence can vanish. If $Z$ is a common cause of $X$ and $Y$, or a relay in a chain $X\to Z\to Y$, then $I(X;Y)>0$ but $I(X;Y|Z)=0$. The variable $Z$ “screens off” one from the other.
  • Dependence can appear. If $X$ and $Y$ are independent and $Z$ depends on both (a collider), then $I(X;Y)=0$ but $I(X;Y|Z)>0$. This is “explaining away”, or Berkson's paradox. The extreme case is the XOR, where $Z=X\oplus Y$ makes $X$ and $Y$ completely determine each other given $Z$.

The difference is called the interaction information, $I(X;Y;Z)=I(X;Y)-I(X;Y|Z)$. It is positive when the three variables are redundant, and negative when they are synergistic. It is the one region of the three-variable information diagram that can be negative.

Why this matters in ML

Conditional independence tests drive causal-discovery algorithms. Feature selection methods such as CMIM and mRMR use $I(F;\text{label}|\text{already selected})$ to avoid picking redundant features. In Section 6, conditional MI measures exactly how much a summary statistic throws away.

5Mutual information as a generalized correlation coefficient

Pearson's correlation $\rho=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}$ is the default measure of association, but it only detects linear trends. Take $X$ symmetric around zero and $Y=X^2$. Then $Y$ is a deterministic function of $X$, yet $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}[X^3]=0$, so $\rho=0$.

Mutual information has no such blind spot. It is zero if and only if the variables are independent, whatever shape the dependence takes.

The Gaussian case: where MI and ρ coincide

For a bivariate Gaussian with correlation $\rho$, the entropies are $h(X)=\frac12\log(2\pi e\sigma_X^2)$ and $h(X,Y)=\frac12\log\big((2\pi e)^2\sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2)\big)$. Plugging into $I=h(X)+h(Y)-h(X,Y)$ gives

$I(X;Y)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\rho^2)$

In the Gaussian world, MI and $|\rho|$ carry exactly the same information, related by a monotone map. Inverting the map for any pair of variables defines Linfoot's informational coefficient of correlation, with $I$ measured in nats:

$r_I=\sqrt{1-e^{-2I}}\in[0,1]$

This equals $|\rho|$ for Gaussians but also responds to nonlinear dependence. It is 0 for independent variables and tends to 1 as the relationship becomes deterministic.

What makes a good dependence measure?

Rényi (1959) proposed a wish list. It asks that the measure be defined for any pair of variables, symmetric, scaled to $[0,1]$, zero only under independence, one for a functional relationship, invariant under strictly monotone transformations of either variable, and equal to $|\rho|$ for bivariate normals. Pearson fails several of these items. MI, after the transform above, satisfies all of them.

The invariance deserves emphasis. Replace $Y$ by $e^Y$ and Pearson's $\rho$ changes, because the relationship is no longer a straight line. The mutual information does not change at all, because $e^Y$ carries the same information as $Y$.

Caveats

  • No sign. MI says that variables are dependent, but not whether the trend goes up or down.
  • Hard to estimate. From samples, you must estimate densities or discretize. Simple histogram (“plug-in”) estimates are biased upward, by roughly $\frac{(B-1)^2}{2N\ln 2}$ bits for $B\times B$ bins and $N$ samples. Better estimators exist, such as k-nearest-neighbour (Kraskov et al.) and variational bounds, but the problem gets harder in high dimensions.
  • No scale invariance for free. Raw MI has no fixed upper limit for continuous variables, which is why normalized versions ($r_I$, NMI) are used for comparison.

6Sufficient statistics

You observe data $X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ drawn from a distribution $p(x|\theta)$ with an unknown parameter $\theta$. A statistic $T(X)$ is any function of the data, in other words a summary. A summary is sufficient if it loses nothing about $\theta$.

The classical definition

$T$ is sufficient for $\theta$ if the conditional distribution of the data given $T$ does not depend on $\theta$:

$p(x\mid T(x)=t,\;\theta)=p(x\mid T(x)=t)$

Once you know $T$, the leftover details of the data are noise as far as $\theta$ is concerned. Equivalently, by the Fisher–Neyman factorization theorem, the likelihood splits as $p(x|\theta)=g(T(x),\theta)\,h(x)$.

Example: coin flips and the number of heads

Flip a coin with heads probability $\theta$ a total of $n$ times, and let $T=\sum_i X_i$ be the number of heads. Any particular sequence with $t$ heads has probability $\theta^t(1-\theta)^{n-t}$, and there are $\binom{n}{t}$ such sequences, so $P(T=t\mid\theta)=\binom{n}{t}\theta^t(1-\theta)^{n-t}$. Dividing,

$P(\text{this sequence}\mid T=t,\theta)=\frac{\theta^t(1-\theta)^{n-t}}{\binom{n}{t}\theta^t(1-\theta)^{n-t}}=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{t}}$

The $\theta$ cancels. Given the number of heads, every arrangement is equally likely whatever $\theta$ is, so the order of the flips says nothing about $\theta$.

The information-theoretic definition

Treat $\theta$ as random with any prior. Since $T$ is a function of $X$, the chain $\theta\to X\to T(X)$ is always a Markov chain, and the data processing inequality gives $I(\theta;T)\le I(\theta;X)$. Sufficiency is exactly the case of equality:

$T\text{ sufficient}\iff I(\theta;T(X))=I(\theta;X)\iff\theta\to T(X)\to X\text{ is Markov}$

The proof is the chain rule from Section 4, applied twice to $I(\theta;X,T)$. Because $T$ is a function of $X$, the pair $(X,T)$ carries the same information about $\theta$ as $X$ alone:

$I(\theta;X)=I(\theta;X,T)=I(\theta;T)+I(\theta;X\mid T)$

So the information lost by summarizing is exactly the conditional mutual information $I(\theta;X|T)$. A statistic is sufficient precisely when that conditional MI is zero.

Minimal sufficiency and the bottleneck

The full data is trivially sufficient, so you want the coarsest sufficient summary. A minimal sufficient statistic is a function of every other sufficient statistic. It is the most compressed summary that still loses nothing. In information-bottleneck language, it minimizes $I(X;T)$ subject to $I(\theta;T)=I(\theta;X)$. Relaxing that constraint into a trade-off gives the information bottleneck objective from Section 2.
