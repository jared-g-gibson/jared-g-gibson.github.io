Explain it at the level of

Bound optimization

The general recipe. Also called MM: minorize, then maximize.

Imagine you are walking in thick fog, trying to find the very top of a bumpy hill. The ground is too lumpy to work out where the top is.

Here is a trick. Slide a smooth, round hill underneath the bumpy one, so it touches the bumpy hill only where you are standing. It never pokes above the real ground. The top of a smooth hill is easy to find, so walk there.

The smooth hill was never higher than the real one, so the real ground where you landed is at least that high. You went up for real. Now tuck a new smooth hill under your new spot and do it again. Keep going until you can't climb any higher.

Say you want the maximum of a function f(θ), but setting its derivative to zero gives an algebra mess. Bound optimization trades that one hard problem for a series of easy ones.

At your current guess θt, build a simple stand-in function Q(θ | θt), often a parabola, that follows two rules. It never goes above f, and it equals f at θt. Find the peak of Q with ordinary algebra and jump there. Call that spot θt+1.

Then f can't have gone down. At the new spot f is at least as high as Q. The peak of Q is at least as high as Q was where you started. And Q equals f at the start:

f(θt+1) ≥ Q(θt+1 | θt) ≥ Q(θt | θt) = f(θt)

Build a fresh Q at the new spot and repeat. Each round either improves f or stays put.

Bound optimization, also called minorize–maximize (MM), maximizes an objective f(θ) by iterating over surrogates. At the current iterate θt, choose a surrogate Q(θ | θt) with two properties, then maximize it:

Q(θ | θt) ≤ f(θ) for all θ,    Q(θt | θt) = f(θt),    θt+1 = argmaxθ Q(θ | θt)

Monotone ascent follows from a three-term chain:

f(θt+1) ≥ Q(θt+1 | θt) ≥ Q(θt | θt) = f(θt)

Since f − Q ≥ 0 and equals zero at θt, it is minimized there. If both functions are differentiable, that forces ∇Q(θt | θt) = ∇f(θt), so a fixed point of the iteration is a stationary point of f.

The craft is choosing Q so the argmax is cheap. An L-smoothness bound gives a quadratic Q and the update θ + ∇f(θ)/L, which is gradient ascent with step size 1/L (the demo above). Jensen's inequality gives EM. Convexity and tangent-line bounds give iterative scaling, the convex–concave procedure, and more. A tighter bound allows bigger steps; a looser one is safer but slower. For minimization the same idea is called majorize–minimize.

Lower bound

A stand-in that is never too high and touches the truth at one point.

A lower bound is a promise that the real answer is at least this much. If I tell you "I have at least 5 stickers," I might have 5, or 6, or 20, but never 4.

In our hill trick, the smooth hill is a lower bound for the bumpy hill: wherever you look, the real hill is the same height or taller. The smooth hill also has to touch the real hill at the spot where you're standing. That touching keeps the trick honest.

A function Q is a lower bound for f if Q(θ) ≤ f(θ) for every θ. For bound optimization we also want it to touch f at the current guess, Q(θt) = f(θt). Picture a parabola pressed against the underside of a bumpy curve: exact at one point, an underestimate everywhere else.

The gap f − Q is never negative and is zero at the touch point. A bound that hugs the curve closely (a tight bound) allows big steps. A loose bound forces small, safe steps. In the demo above, drag the curvature slider down until the bound pokes above f. The guarantee breaks, and f can drop.

In EM, the lower bound comes from Jensen's inequality. For any distribution q(z) over the latent variables, concavity of log gives

ℓ(θ) = log Σz q(z) p(x, z | θ)q(z) ≥ Σz q(z) log p(x, z | θ)q(z) = F(q, θ)

Here ℓ(θ) = log p(x | θ) is the log-likelihood, the particular f that EM maximizes. The right side F(q, θ) is the evidence lower bound (ELBO), and the gap is exactly a KL divergence:

ℓ(θ) = F(q, θ) + KL( q(z) ‖ p(z | x, θ) )

So F ≤ ℓ because KL ≥ 0, with equality exactly when q(z) = p(z | x, θ). Setting q to the posterior at θt gives a bound in θ that touches ℓ at θt. That bound equals Q(θ | θt) + H(q), where Q(θ | θt) = Ez | x, θt[log p(x, z | θ)] is the usual EM surrogate. The entropy H(q) doesn't depend on θ, so both have the same maximizer.

The EM algorithm

Bound optimization for models with hidden variables. Expectation, then maximization, repeated.

Imagine a pile of dots from two groups, but the labels fell off, and nobody knows where each group's middle is. EM is a two-step dance.

Step one: guess how much each dot belongs to each group. Step two: move each group's middle to where its dots say it should be. Then dance again with the new middles.

Every round the groups fit the dots a little better, or at least no worse. Stop when the dance stops changing anything.

EM stands for Expectation–Maximization. It finds the best settings (parameters) for a model when part of the story is hidden, like which of two groups each data point came from. You can't find the best group centers without knowing who belongs where, and you can't tell who belongs where without knowing the centers.

EM breaks that circle. Pretend the current parameters are right and work out soft group memberships (the E-step). Then pretend the memberships are right and refit the parameters (the M-step). Repeat. The probability of your data goes up or stays the same on every loop.

EM is bound optimization where the lower bound is built from the memberships. One catch: it can settle on a good answer that isn't the best one, so people try several starting points. The demo further down lets you see that happen.

EM maximizes the marginal log-likelihood ℓ(θ) = log Σz p(x, z | θ) when the sum over latent z makes direct optimization hard. It alternates exact coordinate ascent on the ELBO F(q, θ): the E-step maximizes over q with θ fixed, and the M-step maximizes over θ with q fixed (Neal and Hinton, 1998). In the language of bound optimization, the surrogate is F(qt, ·). Monotonicity is the three-term chain again:

ℓ(θt+1) ≥ F(qt, θt+1) ≥ F(qt, θt) = ℓ(θt)

Under regularity conditions the iterates converge to a stationary point of ℓ (Wu, 1983), usually a local maximum, at a linear rate set by the fraction of missing information. In practice, watch for three things: sensitivity to initialization (use restarts or k-means); degenerate Gaussian-mixture solutions where a component collapses onto one point (σ → 0 sends ℓ → ∞); and an intractable E-step, which calls for variational or Monte Carlo EM.

The E-step

Guess the hidden labels. This builds the lower bound.

The E-step is the guessing step. Each dot gets a score for each group, like "this dot is 80% group A and 20% group B." Dots close to a group's middle get a high score for that group. Dots in between get split scores.

You don't force a dot into one group yet. Partial credit is fine. These guesses are what let us build the smooth hill that touches the real one.

E stands for Expectation. For every data point and every group, use the current parameters and Bayes' rule to compute the probability that the point came from that group. For a mixture of bell curves, the responsibility of group k for point xi is:

γik = (size of group k) × (how well group k's bell curve explains xi)the same quantity added up over all groups

Each responsibility is between 0 and 1, and the responsibilities for one point add up to 1. The E-step changes no parameters. It only turns the current parameters into soft labels, and those soft labels define the lower bound.

The E-step sets qt(z) = p(z | x, θt), the unique maximizer of F(·, θt). This closes the gap, KL = 0, so the bound touches ℓ at θt. It is called "E" because it computes the expectation Q(θ | θt) = Ez | x, θt[log p(x, z | θ)]; in practice you only need the expected sufficient statistics. For a Gaussian mixture the posterior is the responsibility:

γik = πk N(xi | μk, σ2k)Σj πj N(xi | μj, σ2j)

When the posterior is intractable, restrict q to a tractable family and maximize F over that family (variational EM). The bound then no longer touches ℓ, so F still increases monotonically but ℓ is no longer guaranteed to.

The M-step

Refit the parameters using the guesses. This climbs the bound.

The M-step is the improving step. Now treat the guesses as if they were true and slide each group's middle to its best spot: the average of its dots, where a dot with a bigger score counts for more. Also adjust how wide each group is, and how many dots it seems to own.

Finding the best spot for the smooth hill is the climb to its top. Because the smooth hill sits under the real one, the real hill goes up too.

M stands for Maximization. Treat the responsibilities as fixed weights and find the parameters that fit the data best given those weights. For a bell-curve group the best answers are weighted averages. With weights wi = γik, the new center is:

μk = Σ wi xiΣ wi

The new spread comes from the weighted average of squared distances to that center, and the new group size is the group's total weight divided by the number of points. Because the weights are fixed, this is the easy problem and it has a formula. It is the jump to the peak of the easy hill, so the real function can't go down.

The M-step sets θt+1 = argmaxθ Q(θ | θt). With γ held fixed, the expected complete-data log-likelihood separates across components, and each component is a weighted maximum-likelihood problem. For a Gaussian mixture with n points and nk = Σi γik:

πk = nkn     μk = Σi γik xink     σ2k = Σi γik (xi − μk)2nk

For exponential-family complete-data models, the M-step touches the data only through expected sufficient statistics, so it is the ordinary maximum-likelihood estimate with counts replaced by expected counts. Generalized EM only asks for Q(θt+1 | θt) ≥ Q(θt | θt), for example one gradient step on Q, and keeps the monotone guarantee.

Why the likelihood never goes down

Three facts carry the whole argument. This part is written at the undergraduate level.

The bound sits under ℓ and has a known gap

ℓ(θ) = F(q, θ) + KL( q(z) ‖ p(z | x, θ) )   ≥   F(q, θ)

E-step: make the bound touch

Choose qt(z) = p(z | x, θt). The KL term is zero at θt, so F(qt, θt) = ℓ(θt). In the demo, this is why the blue curve meets the black one at s = 0.

M-step: climb the bound

Choose θt+1 to maximize F(qt, ·). Then F(qt, θt+1) ≥ F(qt, θt). In the demo, this is the peak of the blue curve at s = 1.

Put them together

ℓ(θt+1) ≥ F(qt, θt+1) ≥ F(qt, θt) = ℓ(θt)

The readouts in the EM demo track the exact bookkeeping. Subtracting ℓ(θt) = F(qt, θt) from the decomposition at θt+1 gives:

ℓ(θt+1) − ℓ(θt) = [ F(qt, θt+1) − F(qt, θt) ] + KL( qt ‖ p(z | x, θt+1) )

That is "ℓ gained = bound gained + gap opened." Both terms are nonnegative, which is the monotone guarantee. The next E-step closes the new gap and the cycle repeats.
