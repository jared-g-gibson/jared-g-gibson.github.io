Kernel density estimation

Draw a smooth curve that shows where your data tends to live, without deciding in advance what shape the curve has.

Elementary

Imagine your whole class lines up along a number line by height. Some kids stand in clumps and some stand alone. You want to draw a picture of where most of the kids are.

Here is the trick. Every kid wears a little soft hill on their head, and all the hills are the same size. Where lots of kids stand close together, their hills pile up into one tall mountain. Where kids are far apart, the ground stays low.

Add up all the little hills and you get one smooth, hilly picture of the whole class. That picture is a kernel density estimate. Tall parts mean "lots of kids around here." Try the demo: drag the width slider and watch the hills fatten and shrink.

High school

A common question in statistics is: if I pick a random value, which values are most likely? A histogram answers by counting points in bins, but bins have hard edges, and the picture changes if you slide where the bins start.

Kernel density estimation (KDE) swaps each data point for a small smooth hill and then averages the hills. Every hill has an area of 1, and dividing by the number of points N keeps the total area at 1, which is what a probability density must have. A taller curve means "more likely."

The width of the hills is called the bandwidth, written h. It controls how smooth the final curve is. KDE is nonparametric: we never assume the data follow a bell curve or any other named shape.

Undergraduate

Given i.i.d. samples x1, …, xN, the kernel density estimate places one scaled kernel on every data point and averages:

p̂(x) = 1N NΣn=1 Kh(x − xn)Kh is a density kernel with bandwidth h (topic 2)

With a Gaussian kernel this is exactly a mixture model with N components, one centered on each data point, equal weights 1/N and variance h2:

p̂(x) = NΣn=1 1N 𝒩(x | xn, h2)

Unlike a Gaussian mixture model there is no EM and no number of components to choose. "Training" means storing the data (memory-based learning), so the model grows with N and each evaluation costs O(N). Since K is a density, p̂ ≥ 0 and ∫p̂ = 1 automatically.

The estimator is biased but consistent. Its mean is E[p̂(x)] = (Kh ∗ p)(x), a smoothed copy of the true density, and its variance is roughly p(x) ∫K2 / (N h). Large h raises bias and lowers variance, small h does the reverse. Topic 4 picks h.

Density kernels

The shape of the little hill each data point gets.

Elementary

A kernel is the shape of the hill we put on each kid. It could be a smooth bell, a flat box, or a rounded roof.

There are two rules. First, the hill looks the same on its left side and its right side. Second, it holds exactly one "pancake" of stuff, so the total area under it is 1, no matter how wide or tall it is.

Here is a surprise. Switching between hill shapes barely changes the final picture. Making the hills wider or skinnier changes it a lot. Try the checkboxes in the demo and see for yourself.

High school

A density kernel is a function K(u) that is never negative, is symmetric (K(−u) = K(u)) and has area exactly 1 under it. Four common choices:

  • Gaussian: the bell curve. It never quite reaches zero, so every point has at least a tiny say everywhere.
  • Boxcar: a flat rectangle. It simply counts the points inside a window.
  • Epanechnikov: an upside-down parabola. Points at the edge of the window count for nothing.
  • Tricube: a rounded cap that flattens smoothly to zero at the edges.

The bandwidth rescales a kernel: Kh(x) = K(x/h) / h. Dividing the argument by h stretches the hill sideways. Dividing the result by h squashes it down so its area stays 1.

Undergraduate

A smoothing kernel is a function K: ℝ → ℝ+ with ∫K(u)du = 1 and K(−u) = K(u). This is a different object from a Mercer kernel (a positive-definite similarity used in SVMs and Gaussian processes), although a stationary Mercer kernel can often serve as a smoothing kernel too. The bandwidth enters as

Kh(u) = 1h K(uh)in D dimensions the prefactor is h−D

The Epanechnikov kernel minimizes the asymptotic mean integrated squared error among nonnegative kernels, but the others lose only a few percent of efficiency. In practice h matters far more than K. One trap: the same h does not mean the same effective width. The standard deviation of Kh is h for the Gaussian, h/√3 for the boxcar and h/√5 for Epanechnikov, so compact kernels need a larger h to look equally smooth. Compact support also gives exact zeros, which makes sparse and tree-based evaluation possible.

Parzen window density estimator

Slide a window along the line and count who is inside.

Elementary

Picture a cardboard frame you can slide along a line of kids. At every spot you count how many kids fit inside the frame. Many kids means a tall mark. Few kids means a short mark.

Slide the frame all the way along, drawing a mark at each spot, and you get a picture of where the kids crowd together. A wide frame gives a blurry picture. A skinny frame gives a jumpy one.

That is the Parzen window idea: count the kids in a sliding frame. Press the sweep button in the demo to watch it draw.

High school

Take the boxcar kernel: a window reaching a distance h to each side of a spot x. Let n(x) be the number of data points inside it. The density estimate is

p̂(x) = n(x)N · 2h

The top is the number of points in the window and N is the total, so n(x)/N is the fraction of the data in the window. Dividing by the window's width 2h turns "fraction" into "fraction per unit of length," which is what a density is.

Sliding this window along the axis is like a histogram whose bins follow you around, with no fixed edges. Swap the sharp window for a smooth Gaussian one and each point gets a gradual say instead of an all-or-nothing vote.

Undergraduate

The Parzen window (Parzen–Rosenblatt) estimator is the KDE of topic 1. For the boxcar kernel K(u) = ½ · 1(|u| ≤ 1) it reduces to a windowed count:

p̂(x) = 12hN · #{ n : |x − xn| ≤ h }

This estimate is piecewise constant. It jumps by 1/(2hN) whenever a point enters or leaves the window, so it is not smooth and not differentiable. A smoother kernel, such as the Gaussian, gives a smooth p̂ and the mixture form from topic 1.

In D dimensions use Kh(u) = h−D K(u/h), with a radial or product kernel. The best achievable MISE rate degrades to N−4/(D+4), the curse of dimensionality: the sample size needed for a given accuracy grows exponentially in D. Fitting costs nothing and evaluating costs O(N), which is why practical implementations use KD-trees, ball trees or FFT-based binning.

How to choose the bandwidth

The width h is the one setting that really changes the answer.

Elementary

How wide should each little hill be? If the hills are too skinny, every kid gets their own spike and the picture looks like a jagged mountain range. If the hills are too fat, they melt into one big blob and you can't see the clumps anymore. You want the "just right" width, like Goldilocks.

There are two ways to find it. One is a rule of thumb: look at how spread out the kids are and how many there are, then use a recipe. The other is a hide-and-guess game: hide one kid, draw the picture from everyone else, and see how well the picture predicts where the hidden kid stands. Do that for every kid and pick the width that guesses best.

Try both buttons in the demo. Then change the group to "two clumps" and see which method does better.

High school

The bandwidth trades two kinds of error against each other. A small h follows the data closely, so the curve wiggles around every random bump (high variance, "overfitting"). A large h smooths so much that real features, like two separate peaks, disappear (high bias, "oversmoothing").

Rule of thumb (Silverman). If the data are roughly bell-shaped, a good choice is about 1.06 × (spread) × N−1/5. More data lets you use narrower hills, but slowly, because of the fifth root. A robust version measures spread with the median absolute deviation (MAD), so a few outliers don't inflate h.

Cross-validation. For each point, build the estimate from the other N − 1 points and score how likely the held-out point is. Add up the logs of those scores and pick the h with the best total.

The rule of thumb assumes a bell shape, so it tends to oversmooth data with two peaks. Cross-validation makes no such assumption.

Undergraduate

Plug-in rule. The asymptotic MISE of a KDE is

AMISE(h) = R(K)N h + 14 h4 μ2(K)2 R(p″)R(g) = ∫g², μ₂(K) = ∫u²K(u)du · variance term falls with h, bias term grows with h

Minimizing over h gives h* ∝ N−1/5 and MISE ∝ N−4/5. If the unknown p is replaced by a Gaussian 𝒩(μ, σ2) and K is Gaussian, then R(p″) = 3/(8√π σ5) and the rule becomes

h = σ̂ (43N)1/5 ≈ 1.06 σ̂ N−1/5robust version: σ̂ = 1.4826 · MAD, MAD = mediann |xn − median(x)|

The constant 1.4826 equals 1/Φ−1(3/4) and makes the MAD consistent for σ under normality. Because the Gaussian reference ignores skew and multimodality, this rule oversmooths such data.

Leave-one-out cross-validation. Choose

ĥCV = argmaxh NΣn=1 log p̂−n(xn), p̂−n(x) = 1N − 1 m≠nΣ Kh(x − xm)

Leaving the point out is essential. Maximizing the plain likelihood drives h → 0, where p̂ becomes a comb of spikes on the data. LOO-CV needs no refitting for a KDE and costs O(N2) per candidate h. With a compact kernel the score can be −∞ when a held-out point has no neighbor in its window, so a Gaussian kernel is the safer choice here.

From KDE to KNN classification

A majority vote among neighbors is a density estimate in disguise.

Elementary

On a playground there are kids in red shirts, orange shirts and green shirts, standing in groups. A new kid walks in and you can't see his shirt. What color is he probably wearing?

Ask the K kids standing closest to him. If 4 of the 5 nearest kids wear red, guess red. This is the K-nearest neighbors game.

Here is the secret. It's the hill-picture idea again. Draw a hill picture for each color, and ask which color's picture is tallest where the new kid stands. In the demo, a circle grows around the new kid until it holds exactly K other kids, and the count of each color inside it tells you the guess.

High school

To classify a new point there are two natural plans.

  • Vote. Find the K closest labeled points and let them vote. This is KNN.
  • Compare crowds. Estimate how crowded each class is around the point, weigh each class by how common it is overall, and pick the class with the biggest result.

These are the same plan when the "crowd" is measured in a circle that grows just big enough to hold K neighbors. The circle is small where the data are dense and large where they are sparse, so it adapts automatically. The probability of class c comes out as

p(class c) = number of the K neighbors in class cK

A fixed-width version, the KDE classifier, uses the same recipe but with hills of a fixed width instead of a circle that grows. Switch between the two in the demo and compare the regions.

Undergraduate

Build a generative classifier whose class-conditional densities come from a nonparametric estimator. Grow a ball around x until it contains K points of any class. Let V(x) be its volume and Kc(x) the number of class-c points inside, so Σc Kc(x) = K. This is a KDE with a uniform kernel whose width adapts to the local sample density. Then

p(x | y=c) = Kc(x)Nc V(x), p(y=c) = NcN, p(x) = KN V(x)

and by Bayes' rule the volume cancels:

p(y=c | x) = p(x | y=c) p(y=c)p(x) = Kc(x)K

So the KNN rule is the Bayes classifier under a K-nearest-neighbor density estimate. That density estimate is improper: K/(N V(x)) has heavy tails and its integral diverges. The classifier never needs the integral, only ratios, so this does no harm.

With a fixed-bandwidth class-conditional KDE p̂(x|c) = Nc−1 Σn∈c Kh(x − xn) the same derivation gives

p(y=c | x) = Σn: yn=c Kh(x − xn)NΣn=1 Kh(x − xn)a kernel-weighted vote · the classification analogue of Nadaraya-Watson (topic 7) with one-hot labels

If K → ∞ and K/N → 0 as N → ∞, the KNN rule is consistent for the Bayes classifier. For K = 1, the Cover–Hart bound says its asymptotic error is at most twice the Bayes error.

Kernel regression

Estimate the joint density of (x, y) with a KDE, then slice it.

Elementary

Now every kid has two numbers: their age (call it x) and how many jumping jacks they can do (call it y). Put each kid on a map as a dot, with age going across and jumping jacks going up.

Give each dot a round hill, and add the hills up. You get a hilly map showing where dots like to live, a bit like a mountain range.

Now you meet a new kid who is 9. To guess his jumping jacks, walk up the line that says "age = 9" and look at how high the ground is as you go. The ground is highest where kids of that age usually land. The average spot along the line is your guess. Drag the line in the demo to try it.

High school

Regression means predicting a number y from another number x. Here we do it with no formula assumed in advance.

Step one: use a KDE on the pairs (x, y). Each data point gets a round 2D hill, and the sum is a hilly surface showing where pairs are likely.

Step two: fix the input at some value x0 and slice through the surface along the line x = x0. The height along the slice, once rescaled so its area is 1, is a curve over y showing which outputs are likely for that input. Its average is the prediction.

That average turns out to be a weighted average of the training y values, where training points with x near x0 get more weight. The next topic writes that out.

Undergraduate

Regression targets the conditional mean E[y | x]. A KDE gives it without any parametric form for the relationship. Model the joint density with a product kernel:

p̂(x, y) = 1N NΣn=1 Kh(x − xn) Kh(y − yn)

Integrating over y gives the marginal p̂(x) = N−1 Σn Kh(x − xn), because ∫Kh(y − yn)dy = 1. The conditional density is then

p̂(y | x) = p̂(x, y)p̂(x) = NΣn=1 wn(x) Kh(y − yn), wn(x) = Kh(x − xn)Σn′ Kh(x − xn′)

This is a mixture over y with one component per training point, and the mixing weights wn(x) depend on the query and sum to 1. Taking its mean, with ∫y Kh(y − yn)dy = yn by symmetry, gives E[y|x] = Σn wn(x) yn, the Nadaraya-Watson estimator of the next topic.

The kernel width used for y can differ from the one used for x. It leaves the mean unchanged and only enters the variance (topic 8). The demo ties the two together, as in the product kernel above.

Nadaraya-Watson estimator for the mean

A weighted average where closeness decides the weight.

Elementary

You want to guess how many jumping jacks a 9-year-old can do. So you ask every kid in your data, but you listen to some voices louder than others. A 9-year-old's answer is very loud. A 4-year-old's answer is almost silent.

Then you average the answers, and the louder voices count more. That is the whole method.

If you only listen to the single closest kid, your guess jumps around from kid to kid. If you listen to everybody the same, you just get the class average no matter who is asking. The sweet spot is in between. In the demo, each dot grows when its voice gets louder.

High school

To predict at an input x, take a weighted average of all the training outputs:

f̂(x) = w1 y1 + w2 y2 + … + wN yN

Each weight comes from a kernel score of how near xn is to x, rescaled so that all the weights add up to 1. Because it is a weighted average, the prediction always lies between the smallest and largest yn; it can't overshoot.

The bandwidth h decides how far "near" reaches. As h shrinks toward 0 the prediction copies the nearest point's y and the curve becomes a staircase. As h grows very large every weight becomes equal and the curve flattens to the overall average of y. In between you get a smooth curve through the middle of the data.

Undergraduate

From topic 6, the conditional mean of the joint KDE is the Nadaraya-Watson (NW) estimator, also called a kernel smoother:

f̂(x) = NΣn=1 wn(x) yn, wn(x) = Kh(x − xn)NΣn′=1 Kh(x − xn′)

It is also the solution of a locally constant weighted least-squares problem, f̂(x) = argminμ Σn Kh(x − xn)(yn − μ)2, which topic 9 generalizes to local lines. The weights are a similarity-based, normalized lookup into stored labels, so this is memory-based learning with O(N) cost per query.

Limits in h: with a Gaussian kernel, h → 0 gives 1-nearest-neighbor regression (piecewise constant), and h → ∞ gives the global mean ȳ. For smooth f and design density p, the interior asymptotics are

Bias ≈ h2 μ2(K) [ f″(x)2 + f′(x) p′(x)p(x) ], Var ≈ σ2(x) R(K)N h p(x)

The bias contains a design-dependent term f′p′/p, and near the edges of the data it degrades to O(h), because the window is lopsided. The optimal rate is h ∝ N−1/5. Both defects are repaired by the local linear fit in topic 9.

A connection worth knowing: with a Gaussian kernel, wn(x) ∝ exp(−‖x − xn‖2/2h2) is a softmax over training points. NW regression is therefore attention with fixed keys xn, values yn and a squared-distance score.

8

Estimator for the variance

How spread out are the y values near x?

Elementary

Our guess says a 9-year-old does 20 jumping jacks. But how sure are we? If kids around that age do between 19 and 21, we feel confident. If they do anywhere from 5 to 40, the guess is shaky.

We can measure that "spread-out-ness" with the same louder-and-quieter voices. The shaded ribbon in the demo shows it. The ribbon is skinny where kids all do about the same thing, and wide where they are all over the place.

High school

Variance measures spread: it is the average of (value − mean)2. To get it at a particular x, reuse the same weights as the weighted average. Weight each squared gap (yn − μ(x))2 and add them up, then add a small extra amount σ2 that accounts for the blur of the hills themselves.

The ribbon μ(x) ± 2√V(x) should contain about 95% of the y values near x when they are roughly bell-shaped. Where the data are noisier, the ribbon is wider. Where few training points are nearby, the estimate is unstable, so take the edges of the ribbon with a grain of salt.

Undergraduate

The conditional density from topic 6, p̂(y|x) = Σn wn(x) K(y − yn), also gives the predictive variance. Let the kernel in the y direction have variance σ2. Then E[y2|x] = Σn wn(x)(σ2 + yn2), and

V̂[y | x] = E[y2|x] − μ̂(x)2 = σ2 + NΣn=1 wn(x) yn2 − μ̂(x)2 = σ2 + NΣn=1 wn(x) (yn − μ̂(x))2μ̂(x) is the NW mean from topic 7 · the last step uses Σ wn = 1

The first term is the spread added by smoothing in y. The second is the weighted sample variance of nearby targets. It is the total variance of a mixture: within-component variance σ2 plus between-component spread of the means.

Two remarks. First, this is the variance of the predictive distribution of y (aleatoric spread), not the uncertainty of the estimated mean μ̂(x), which shrinks like 1/(N h). Second, a common alternative smooths squared residuals, V̂(x) = Σn wn(x)(yn − f̂(xn))2. It avoids the extra σ2 and captures heteroscedastic noise directly, but it needs a good first-stage fit f̂.

Locally weighted regression

Fit a small straight line at every spot, and trust the nearby points more.

Elementary

Last time we averaged the jumping jacks of kids near age 9. Here is a smarter move. Look at the dots near age 9 and draw the best straight line through them. Kids closer to age 9 get a bigger say in where the line goes. Then read your guess off the line at age 9.

Why is a line better than an average? Think about the oldest kids on the chart. All their neighbors are younger, so a plain average gets dragged down. A line can see the dots are going up and keep going up.

You draw a brand new line for every age. In the demo, the short straight piece is the line for the spot you picked.

High school

In locally weighted regression (LOWESS or LOESS), the prediction at an input x0 comes from a straight-line fit to just the nearby data. The fit is a weighted least-squares line: it minimizes the sum of weight × (error)2, with large weights for points close to x0. The height of that line at x0 is the prediction. Then you repeat for the next x0.

The weighted average from topic 7 is the same idea but with a flat line, which only has a height. A sloped line also captures the trend, so it does better at the edges of the data and on steep parts of the curve.

The price is more computing, since each prediction solves a small least-squares problem. Setting the degree to 0 in the demo gives back the weighted average.

Undergraduate

NW is a locally constant fit. Locally weighted regression fits a local polynomial: at each query x*, solve a kernel-weighted least-squares problem

β̂(x*) = argminβ NΣn=1 Kh(x* − xn) (yn − βT φ(xn))2 = (ΦT D Φ)−1 ΦT D yΦ is the N×p design matrix · D = diag(Kh(x* − x1), …, Kh(x* − xN)) · prediction f̂(x*) = β̂(x*)T φ(x*)

Here φ(x) = (1, x) gives the local linear fit and φ(x) = (1, x, x2) the local quadratic. Taking φ(x) = 1 reduces (ΦTDΦ)−1ΦTDy to Σ wn yn, which is exactly NW.

The result is still a linear smoother, f̂(x*) = Σn ln(x*) yn, where the equivalent weights ln sum to 1 but can be negative. Local linear regression has bias O(h2) everywhere, including the boundary, and the bias no longer contains the design term f′p′/p. Its variance is of the same order as NW's. This design adaptivity is why local linear is the default. Higher degrees reduce bias further at a cost in variance, and odd degrees are generally preferred.

Cleveland's LOWESS uses a tricube kernel with an adaptive bandwidth set by a fraction of the nearest neighbors, plus a few robustness iterations that down-weight points with large residuals. The method is lazy: nothing is fit until a query arrives, and each query costs O(N) to weight plus a p × p solve.
