K‑Nearest Neighbor Classification

Label a new example by letting its closest labeled examples vote.

Elementary school

Imagine you are new at school and you don’t know which lunch table to join. So you look at the 3 kids sitting closest to you. Two of them are in the chess club and one is in the art club. Your best guess? You are probably a chess kid!

That is k‑nearest neighbors. The computer keeps a big pile of examples that already have names. When something new shows up, it finds the k examples that look most like it, lets them vote, and goes with the winner. The letter k is just how many neighbors get a vote.

High school senior

KNN stores every labeled example it has ever seen. To classify a new point, it measures the distance from that point to every stored point, picks the k closest, and takes a majority vote. Distance is usually the straight‑line (Euclidean) distance you know from the Pythagorean theorem.

The choice of k matters. With k = 1 the model copies its single nearest neighbor, so one mislabeled point can make a strange island in the map. With a large k the boundaries get smooth, but small groups can get outvoted and disappear. KNN has no training step at all (it is a “lazy learner”): all of the work happens when you ask it a question.

Undergraduate

Given training pairs (xi, yi) and a metric d, let Nk(x) be the k training points closest to x. The classifier is a plurality vote, optionally weighted by wi = 1/d(x, xi)2:

ŷ(x) = argmaxc Σi ∈ Nk(x) wi · 𝟙[yi = c]
  • Bias–variance in k. Small k gives low bias and high variance (jagged boundary). Large k smooths the boundary and raises bias. Pick k by cross‑validation.
  • Consistency. As N → ∞ with k → ∞ and k/N → 0, KNN approaches the Bayes error. For k = 1 the asymptotic two‑class error is at most 2R*(1 − R*), where R* is the Bayes error (Cover and Hart, 1967).
  • The metric is the model. Euclidean distance treats every feature equally, so unscaled features dominate. A Mahalanobis metric dM(x, x′) = √((x − x′)T M (x − x′)) with M ⪰ 0 reweights and rotates the space. Learning M (or a nonlinear map) is metric learning, and LDA gives one particular choice of M.
  • Cost. No training, but each query costs O(N·d) in time and the full dataset stays in memory.

InstrumentVote with your neighbors

Try this. Put the query between two clusters and set k = 1, then k = 25. The prediction can flip, and the shaded regions change from jagged to smooth. Then switch the distance to Manhattan and watch the circle around the neighbors turn into a diamond.

The Curse of Dimensionality

With many features, almost everything is far from everything else, and “nearest” stops meaning much.

Elementary school

Hide a marble somewhere along a short hallway. Easy to find! Now hide it on a big classroom floor. Harder. Now hide it in a giant room where you can also go up, and up‑up, and sideways‑sideways, in a hundred directions at once. There is so much room that your toys end up very far away from each other.

When everything is far away from everything, “the closest friend” is hardly closer than anybody else. That is the curse: the more directions you have, the less “nearby” means.

High school senior

Every new feature adds a new direction (a new axis). To keep your data equally crowded, you need exponentially more of it: ten points per axis is 10 points in 1‑D, 100 in 2‑D, and 1010 in 10‑D.

There is a second, stranger effect. When you pick random points in a high‑dimensional box, the distances between them all bunch up around the same value. The nearest neighbor and the farthest neighbor end up almost the same distance away, so “closest” carries very little information. The instrument below shows this happening.

Undergraduate
  • Sparsity. To capture a fraction r of the unit hypercube with a sub‑cube you need edge length r1/d. For r = 0.1: 0.79 at d = 10, 0.977 at d = 100. “Local” neighborhoods are no longer local.
  • Distance concentration. For i.i.d. coordinates, (dmax − dmin) / dmin → 0 in probability as d → ∞ (Beyer et al., 1999). For uniform points on [0,1]d, E‖x − y‖² = d/6, while the relative spread of the distance shrinks like 1/√d.
  • Geometry. The volume of the unit ball, Vd = πd/2 / Γ(d/2 + 1), tends to 0, and a fraction 1 − (1 − ε)d of it lies within ε of the surface. Mass lives in the shell and in the corners.
(dmax − dmin) / dmin → 0   as   d → ∞

Ways out. Real data usually lies near a low‑dimensional manifold (the manifold hypothesis), so reduce dimension (PCA, LDA, random projections), use a learned embedding (the whole point of deep metric learning), try L1 or fractional norms, or use cosine similarity on well‑normalized features.

InstrumentWatch distances bunch up

200 random points are drawn inside a unit box with the number of dimensions you choose, plus one random query. The left chart shows how far each point is from the query, as a multiple of the average distance.

Try this. Start at d = 1 and slide to the right. The histogram turns from a wide spread into a narrow spike, and the farthest ÷ nearest ratio falls toward 1. Watch the “side length needed” bar: to catch just 10% of the data you must span almost the full range of every axis.

Reducing Speed and Memory Requirements

Brute‑force KNN compares against everything it has ever stored. There are three ways to cheat well.

Elementary school

If you had to ask every kid in the whole school “who looks most like me?”, it would take forever. And you would have to remember every single face!

Computers use shortcuts. They can remember only the most helpful kids (the ones standing at the edges of each group). They can keep a smaller picture of each kid, like a tiny thumbnail. And they can ask only the kids in the right hallway instead of the whole school. The answer might be a tiny bit wrong sometimes, but it comes super fast.

High school senior

Brute‑force KNN compares the query to all N stored points, each with d numbers. That means N × d work for every question and N × d numbers to keep in memory. Three families of fixes:

  • Store fewer points. Throw away examples deep inside a class and keep the ones near the boundary (“prototype selection” or “condensing”).
  • Store fewer or smaller numbers. Shrink each point with dimensionality reduction like PCA, or save each number with less precision (for example 8 bits instead of 32).
  • Search smarter. Organize points into a tree, a grid or a graph so you only check candidates near the query. This is approximate nearest neighbor search: it trades a little accuracy for a big speedup.

Both instruments below let you see the trade.

Undergraduate

Fewer points (reduce N)

  • Hart’s condensed NN (1968) keeps a subset S that classifies the rest correctly under 1‑NN. Wilson editing (ENN) removes points whose k neighbors disagree with them (noise), and is often run first.
  • Cluster centroids (k‑means) as prototypes; learning vector quantization.

Fewer or cheaper dimensions (reduce d)

  • PCA or LDA (at most C − 1 dimensions). Random projections: by Johnson–Lindenstrauss, m = O(ε−2 log N) dimensions preserve all pairwise distances within a factor 1 ± ε.
  • Learned embeddings (topics 5–7) map a 150,000‑pixel image to roughly 128 numbers. This is the largest saving of all.
  • Quantization: float32 → float16 or int8 (2–4× smaller); product quantization splits a vector into m parts and stores one 8‑bit codebook index per part (m bytes per vector); binary codes use Hamming distance via popcount.

Smarter search (reduce work per query)

  • Exact trees (k‑d tree, ball tree) work well up to roughly 20 dimensions, then degrade toward brute force: the curse again.
  • Approximate: locality‑sensitive hashing; inverted files (IVF: cluster, then search only the nprobe nearest clusters); graph search (HNSW, about O(log N) per query). Libraries such as FAISS combine these (for instance IVF + PQ).
IVF cost per query ≈ nlist + nprobe · N / nlist   distance computationsCompare nlist centroids, then scan the nprobe closest lists. Larger nprobe raises recall and cost together.

Open Set Recognition

Classify the things you know, and say “I’ve never seen that” about the things you don’t.

Elementary school

Suppose you have only ever seen cats and dogs. Then you meet a giraffe! One kid says, “It’s a dog!” because a dog is the closest thing they know. A smarter kid says, “I have never seen this before. It’s something new.”

Open set recognition teaches computers to be the smarter kid. They name the things they know, and they say “I don’t know” when something is too different from everything they have seen.

High school senior

An ordinary (“closed set”) classifier assumes every test example belongs to a class it saw in training. Real life is not like that: a new person walks up to a door camera, or a new species shows up in a wildlife photo. A closed‑set model has to pick a known label anyway, so it is confidently wrong.

With nearest neighbors there is a simple fix. Measure the distance to the closest known example. If it is farther than a threshold, answer “unknown”. The threshold is a trade‑off: a strict one catches nearly every stranger but also rejects some genuine members of known classes, and a loose one does the opposite. Slide the threshold in the instrument and watch both rates change.

Undergraduate

Let 𝒦 be the training classes. The test distribution may contain classes outside 𝒦, and the goal is a map f : 𝒳 → 𝒦 ∪ {unknown} that keeps open space risk low, meaning it does not label regions far from any training data as known (Scheirer et al., 2013).

f(x) = ŷ(x) if mini d(φ(x), φ(xi)) ≤ τ,   otherwise unknown
  • Why softmax is a weak unknown detector. Probabilities must sum to one, and ReLU networks are piecewise linear, so confidence can grow along a ray far from all training data. High softmax confidence does not imply “near known data”.
  • Why metric learning fits. Distances in an embedding φ give a natural novelty score: nearest‑neighbor distance, distance to a class mean or prototype, or a Mahalanobis distance. New identities can be enrolled by storing embeddings, with no retraining (face recognition, person re‑identification).
  • Other scores: OpenMax (Bendale and Boult, 2016), EVT/Weibull calibration (W‑SVM), energy score, max‑logit. Vaze et al. (2022) showed that a strong closed‑set classifier with a simple max‑logit score is a very competitive open‑set baseline.
  • Evaluation: AUROC of known vs. unknown, TPR at a fixed FPR, OSCR, or open‑set F1. Choose τ on validation data, ideally with some held‑out “known unknowns”. Related but distinct problems: out‑of‑distribution detection and novelty detection (these do not also classify the known classes).

InstrumentSet the “I don’t know” threshold

Solid ○ □ △ are stored, labeled training examples in an embedding space. Hollow ○ □ △ are new known‑class test points. Magenta ◇ are examples of classes the model never saw. Each stored point projects a halo of radius τ: inside any halo means “known”.

Green: known test points correctly accepted. Magenta: unknown points correctly rejected.

Try this. Drag the black diamond (the query) into the empty gap between clusters, then out to the far corner. Next sweep τ from small to large: the magenta curve falls as you accept more strangers while the green curve rises. The best threshold is wherever the two costs balance for your application.

Deep Metric Learning

Train a neural network to draw a map where similar things are close and different things are far.

Elementary school

Imagine a huge table where every kid has to pick a seat. We tell the computer: “Friends should sit close together, and kids who are not friends should sit far apart.” After lots of practice, the computer learns a seating chart that really does put similar kids together.

Once it has learned, a brand‑new kid shows up and the computer can find where they belong, just by asking “who is sitting closest?”, even though it never met that kid before.

High school senior

Metric learning means learning what “close” should mean. A neural network (the embedding function) turns each input, like a photo, into a short list of numbers: a point on a map. We train it so photos of the same thing land near each other and photos of different things land far apart.

Afterwards we can use nearest neighbors on those points. This works even for categories that were not in the training set, because we compare distances instead of choosing from a fixed list of answers. It powers face unlock, “find similar products”, and song matching.

In the instrument, a small network takes 2‑D points and learns a new 2‑D map. Watch the classes pull apart.

Undergraduate

Learn fθ : 𝒳 → ℝD so that the induced distance is small for same‑class pairs and large otherwise:

dθ(x, x′) = ‖fθ(x) − fθ(x′)‖2

This is a valid pseudo‑metric by construction (non‑negative, symmetric, triangle inequality).

  • Classical (linear) case. If f(x) = Lx then dθ is a Mahalanobis distance with M = LTL. LMNN, NCA and ITML learn M; LDA is a closed‑form linear special case that maximizes between‑class over within‑class scatter under Gaussian assumptions.
  • Deep case. fθ is a CNN or transformer, usually followed by L2 normalization onto the unit hypersphere. Three design choices define a method: the loss (topics 6 and 7), the sampling or mining strategy, and the embedding dimension.
  • Evaluation. Recall@K, NMI and mAP@R are computed on held‑out classes (a zero‑shot split). Retrieval quality on unseen classes is the whole point.
  • Caution. Musgrave et al. (“A Metric Learning Reality Check”, 2020) found that many reported gains shrink under fair comparisons, so a strong classification baseline (topic 6) is worth having.

InstrumentTrain an embedding in your browser

A tiny neural network (2 → 24 → 24 → 2) is trained live. Left: the original 2‑D inputs. Right: where the network puts those same points. Solid points are training data; hollow points are held‑out test data the network has never trained on.

Triplet and pairwise losses here use squared distances (as in FaceNet). “Spread ratio” is the average distance between same‑class points divided by the average distance between different‑class points: lower means tighter, better separated clusters. With this much data, plain 5‑NN is already accurate in the raw space, so look at the geometry: the network turns each class into a compact clump.

Try this. Pick “XOR corners”. In the input space each class is split between two opposite corners, so a straight‑line (linear) method such as LDA cannot separate them. Press Train and watch the network pull the two corners of each class into one clump.

Classification Loss

Teach the embedding by playing a guessing game: “which class is this?”

Elementary school

One way to teach a computer the seating chart is to play a guessing game. Show it a picture and ask, “Is this a cat, a dog, or a bird?” If it guesses wrong, we tell it, and it tries to do better next time.

To get good at the game, the computer’s inner “picture map” ends up putting cats near cats and dogs near dogs. The map is a happy side effect of playing the game.

High school senior

Train an ordinary classifier. The network gives each class a score, softmax turns the scores into probabilities that add up to 100%, and the cross‑entropy loss is large when the probability of the correct class is small (the loss is −ln of that probability).

Then remove the last layer and use the layer before it as the embedding. This is simple, stable and fast. The weak spots: the model only learns to separate the training classes, not to make tight clusters, and the last layer grows with the number of classes (a million identities means a million‑row layer).

Modern fixes force the features onto a circle (or sphere) and add an angular margin: the correct class must win by a safety gap. Try the second instrument.

Undergraduate
L = −log [ exp(wyTf + by) / Σc exp(wcTf + bc) ]  ,   ∂L/∂zc = pc − 𝟙[c = y]

Here f = fθ(x) is the penultimate feature (the embedding) and the class weight vectors wc act as learned proxies for each class.

  • Limitation. Softmax cares about linear separability, not compactness. Norms and angles are entangled, so Euclidean or cosine distance on f can be noisy.
  • Normalize, then add a margin. NormFace uses logits s·cos θc. Margin variants penalize the target class: SphereFace (multiplicative angle), CosFace (s·(cos θy − m)) and ArcFace:
Larc = −log [ es·cos(θy + m) / ( es·cos(θy + m) + Σc≠y es·cos θc ) ]
  • Proxy‑based metric learning (Proxy‑NCA, SoftTriple, Proxy‑Anchor) keeps one or more learned proxies per class: O(N·C) cost instead of the O(N²) or O(N³) of pairs and triplets. Zhai and Wu (2019) showed that classification is a strong baseline for deep metric learning.
  • Drawbacks. The proxy matrix grows with C, and it is not directly trained to rank unseen classes.

Instrument ASoftmax and cross‑entropy

Set the three raw scores (logits) the network produced, then say which class is actually correct.

True class

Try this. Make the wrong class’s score larger than the correct one’s and watch the loss jump. Then make the correct score much larger: the loss approaches zero and the gradient (p − target) fades, so the network stops learning from examples it already gets right.

Instrument B · Angular margin on a circle

Features and class weights are normalized, so each class is a direction on a circle. Drag the black dot (or use the slider) to move a feature. Logits are s·cos θ, and the margin version adds m to the true class’s angle before taking the cosine.

Try this. Place the feature a little off the true class direction, so the plain loss is already small. Now raise the margin: the margin loss stays large until the feature sits much closer to its class direction. That extra pressure is what tightens the clusters.

Ranking Loss

Skip the class labels. Train on comparisons: who should be closer to whom?

Elementary school

Instead of asking “what is it?”, we ask “who is closer?” There are a few ways to play:

  • Pairs. Show two pictures. If they are friends, pull them together. If not, push them apart, but only until they are far enough. No need to keep pushing forever.
  • Twins (Siamese network). Two identical copies of the same brain look at the two pictures. Because they are exact twins with the same brain, they treat both pictures fairly.
  • Triplets. Show three: you, a friend, and a stranger. The rule: the friend must be closer to you than the stranger, with a little gap to spare.
  • N‑pairs. You, one friend, and lots of strangers all at once. Your friend has to be the closest of everyone!
High school senior
  • Siamese network. Two identical copies of one network (same weights) each turn one input into a point. We then measure the distance between the two points. Using the same weights guarantees both inputs are treated the same way.
  • Pairwise (contrastive) loss. For a similar pair the loss is the distance squared, which pulls them together. For a different pair the loss is (margin − distance)² while they are closer than the margin, and zero once they are farther. The margin keeps us from pushing strangers away forever.
  • Triplet loss. Take an anchor a, a positive p (same class) and a negative n (different class). The loss is max(0, d(a,p) − d(a,n) + margin). It is zero once the positive is closer than the negative by at least the margin. Most random triplets are already satisfied and teach nothing, so training picks the hard ones.
  • N‑pairs loss. Use one positive and many negatives (one per other class) at once and turn their similarities into probabilities with softmax. The network learns from many negatives per step, which makes training faster.
Undergraduate

Siamese architecture and contrastive loss

A Siamese network applies the same fθ (shared parameters) to both inputs, so gradients from both branches add into one update of θ and the induced distance is symmetric by construction (Bromley et al., 1993; Chopra et al., 2005). With y = 1 for similar pairs (Hadsell, Chopra and LeCun, 2006):

L = y · ½ d² + (1 − y) · ½ max(0, m − d)²Weaknesses: pairs are scored independently, the margin m is in absolute units, there are O(N²) pairs, and most pairs are negatives.

Triplet loss

L = [ ‖f(a) − f(p)‖² − ‖f(a) − f(n)‖² + α ]+FaceNet (Schroff et al., 2015) squares the distances; LMNN (Weinberger and Saul) is the linear ancestor.

It is a relative constraint. There are O(N³) triplets, and most are already satisfied, so mining matters. A negative is hard if d(a,n) < d(a,p), semi‑hard if d(a,p) < d(a,n) < d(a,p) + α, and easy otherwise. Online batch‑hard mining within P×K batches (Hermans et al., 2017) is standard. Using only the very hardest negatives early in training can collapse the embedding, and semi‑hard is the safer start.

N‑pair loss (Sohn, 2016)

A batch holds N (anchor, positive) pairs from N distinct classes. Each anchor treats the other N − 1 positives as negatives:

L = (1/N) Σi log( 1 + Σj≠i exp( fiT fj+ − fiT fi+ ) )This is softmax cross‑entropy over similarities. With N = 2 it is a smoothed triplet loss. An L2 penalty on embeddings keeps norms from growing. The same idea with a temperature τ is InfoNCE / NT‑Xent in contrastive self‑supervised learning (SimCLR).

Other pair‑based losses you will meet: lifted structured loss, multi‑similarity loss, circle loss.

InstrumentPull, push and compare

Choose a loss. Drag the points around the embedding space and watch the loss change. Blue ○ are the anchor and its positive; orange □ are negatives.

A one‑number network f(x) = w·x keeps the idea visible: both branches use the same w, so each branch contributes a piece of the gradient and the two pieces add up. Real Siamese networks do exactly this with millions of weights.

Where LDA Fits

Linear discriminant analysis is metric learning with a straight‑line map and a closed‑form answer.

Elementary school

Imagine shining a flashlight on a bunch of red and blue marbles so their shadows land on a wall. If you hold the flashlight at a bad angle, the red and blue shadows pile on top of each other. If you hold it at a good angle, the red shadows land on one side of the wall and the blue shadows on the other.

LDA is a way of finding that best flashlight angle. Try it in the instrument below!

High school senior

LDA squashes your data onto a line (or a flat sheet) so that the classes end up as separate as possible. It looks for a direction where the class averages are far apart and each class’s own spread is small. That is different from PCA, which only looks for the direction with the most spread overall, and that direction can easily mix the classes together.

After LDA squashes the data, you can classify with the nearest class average or with KNN. Deep metric learning does the same job, but the “squashing” is a neural network, so it can bend and fold space instead of only projecting it.

Undergraduate

With within‑class scatter SW and between‑class scatter SB, Fisher’s criterion chooses the projection W that maximizes

J(W) = tr[ (WT SW W)−1 (WT SB W) ]For two classes: w ∝ SW−1(μ₁ − μ₀). In general, W holds the top eigenvectors of SW−1SB, and there are at most C − 1 useful ones.
  • As metric learning. The induced metric is Mahalanobis with M = WWT. Under Gaussian classes with equal covariance, LDA is Bayes‑optimal and nearest‑class‑mean in the projected space is the matching decision rule.
  • Limits. It is linear, assumes one Gaussian blob per class, produces at most C − 1 dimensions, and needs SW to be invertible (regularize when d is large).
  • Deep metric learning replaces W with a network fθ, replaces J with a classification or ranking loss, and handles classes that are not blobs (the XOR example in topic 5). The same distance‑based machinery (KNN, open‑set thresholds, approximate search) then runs on the learned embedding.

InstrumentFind the best projection direction

Two classes of 2‑D points. Rotate the projection line and watch how well the two classes separate along it. The Fisher score J is the squared gap between class means divided by the within‑class spread, both measured along the line.

Try this. Press “Snap to PCA”: the line follows the long axis of the whole cloud, and the two classes overlap almost completely. Then press “Snap to LDA”: the line tilts to where the class means are far apart relative to the spread, and the classes separate. The curve at the bottom shows J for every direction and peaks at the LDA angle.
