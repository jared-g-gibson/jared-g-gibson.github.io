Gaussian Discriminant Analysis

Classify a new point by asking which class's Gaussian cloud it most plausibly came from. This guide explains the idea at three reading levels and pairs each concept with a lab you can push around.

1What Gaussian Discriminant Analysis is

Picture a playground with three clubs. The soccer kids hang out near the field, the chess kids sit near the tables, and the art kids stay by the paint. Each club stands in a loose blob: lots of kids near the middle and a few wandering toward the edges.

A new kid shows up. You cannot ask which club they like. You can only see where they are standing, so you make a guess in two steps. First, for each club you ask, "How normal would it be for a club kid to stand right here?" Second, you ask which clubs have more kids, because a bigger club is a safer bet. The club with the best answer wins.

That is Gaussian Discriminant Analysis. "Gaussian" is a fancy word for a bell-shaped blob that is crowded in the middle and thin at the edges. "Discriminant" just means telling groups apart.

Suppose every example has two measurements, such as height and weight, and a label such as goalkeeper or striker. A Gaussian is a bell curve. It has a center, the mean, and a width, the standard deviation. With two measurements the bell becomes a hill, and the contour lines of the hill are ovals.

GDA fits one hill to each class using the training examples. To label a new point, it looks up the height of every hill at that point, multiplies each by how common its class is, and picks the tallest. That recipe is Bayes' rule:

P(\text{class}\mid\text{point})\;\propto\;p(\text{point}\mid\text{class})\,P(\text{class})

Because the method describes how each class produces points, it is called a generative classifier. The center and the spread of each hill are all it has to learn.

Let \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and y\in\{1,\dots,K\}. GDA is the generative model

y\sim\mathrm{Categorical}(\boldsymbol\pi),\qquad \mathbf{x}\mid y=k\;\sim\;\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k).

Bayes' rule gives the posterior, and the classifier returns its mode:

p(y=k\mid\mathbf{x})=\frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_k,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_j,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j)},\qquad \hat y=\argmax_{k}\,\delta_k(\mathbf{x})
\delta_k(\mathbf{x})=\log\pi_k-\frac{1}{2}\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k|-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k)

Terms common to all classes, such as -\frac{d}{2}\log 2\pi, cancel in the argmax and are dropped. The three remaining terms are a log-prior, a log-determinant penalty for wide classes, and a squared Mahalanobis distance to the class mean. Every other topic on this page is a choice about how to estimate or constrain \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k.

A discriminative classifier such as logistic regression models p(y\mid\mathbf{x}) directly. GDA models the joint distribution p(\mathbf{x},y), so its maximum-likelihood fit has a closed form and needs no iterative optimizer.

Naming varies by course. Some notes use "GDA" only for the shared-covariance case, which is the model LDA fits. This page uses it for the whole family: QDA, LDA, Gaussian naive Bayes and nearest centroid are all special cases.

1.1Gaussian quadratic boundaries

Now give the clubs different shapes. The soccer kids spread out in a long, skinny stripe along the field. The chess kids huddle in a tight round clump in the middle of that stripe. Where should the border go?

The chess kids only ever stand in their small patch. A kid standing even a little way outside that patch is probably a soccer kid. So the border has to wrap around the chess clump, and it comes out curved, like a circle, an oval or a banana. A curved border like this is called a quadratic boundary.

The border between two classes is the set of points where both are equally likely: the two scaled hills have the same height. Take logs of both sides and each class contributes a squared distance written with its own spread matrix, (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}).

When the two classes have different spreads, these squared terms do not cancel. What is left contains x^{2}, xy and y^{2}, and an equation like that draws a conic section: an ellipse, a parabola, a hyperbola or a pair of lines. The narrow class can win in the middle while the wide class wins on both sides of it.

The one-dimensional version is the easiest to picture. Two bell curves of different widths cross at two points, so the narrow one is taller only between them.

Expanding each score shows what survives when two classes are compared:

\delta_k(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}\mathbf{x}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_k-\frac{1}{2}\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k|+\log\pi_k

The boundary between classes k and l is \delta_k=\delta_l, a quadric:

\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+c=0,\qquad \mathbf{A}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_l^{-1}\right),\qquad \mathbf{b}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_k-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_l^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_l
c=-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_k+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_l^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_l^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_l-\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k|}{|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_l|}+\log\frac{\pi_k}{\pi_l}

In two dimensions the eigenvalues of \mathbf{A} classify the curve. Two eigenvalues of the same sign give an ellipse, opposite signs give a hyperbola, exactly one zero eigenvalue gives a parabola, and \mathbf{A}=\mathbf{0} gives a line. Although the boundary is quadratic in \mathbf{x}, the log-odds is linear in the lifted features (\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}).

The price of this flexibility is parameters. A full covariance has d(d+1)/2 free entries per class, so QDA needs K\,d(d+1)/2 of them: 5,050 per class at d=100.

1.2Linear decision boundaries

If every club has the same shape and only stands in a different place, the border turns out straight, like a rope laid across the playground.

Where does the rope go? Halfway between the two club centers, if both clubs have the same number of kids. If one club is bigger, the rope slides toward the smaller club, because the bigger club gets the benefit of the doubt.

The rope is not always at a right angle to the line between the centers. If the blobs are tilted ovals, the rope tilts too.

When both classes use the same spread matrix \boldsymbol{\Sigma}, the squared terms are identical and cancel as soon as you compare the classes. What survives has x and y only to the first power, which is a straight line (a plane in more dimensions):

\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+w_0=0,\qquad \mathbf{w}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)

The line is perpendicular to \mathbf{w}. That is not the same as being perpendicular to the segment joining the two centers. The matrix \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} turns that segment, and leaves it alone only when the spread is round. With equal class sizes the line passes through the midpoint of the centers, and a larger prior for one class pushes the line away from it.

The probability of class 1 follows an S-shaped curve of \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+w_0, the same shape logistic regression uses. GDA gets there by fitting each class's hill first.

With \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k=\boldsymbol{\Sigma} the quadratic terms in \delta_1-\delta_0 cancel and the log-odds is linear:

\log\frac{p(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})}{p(y=0\mid\mathbf{x})}=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+w_0,\qquad \mathbf{w}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)
w_0=-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1+\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)+\log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_0}

So p(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})=\sigma(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+w_0) with \sigma the logistic function, the same functional form as logistic regression. The fits differ. GDA maximizes the joint likelihood p(\mathbf{x},y) in closed form, while logistic regression maximizes the conditional likelihood p(y\mid\mathbf{x}) iteratively. If the Gaussian assumption is right, GDA uses the data more efficiently. If it is wrong, logistic regression is usually the safer choice.

For K classes the boundaries are the pairwise hyperplanes, and the posterior is a softmax of K linear functions of \mathbf{x}.

1.3Model fitting for GDA

To draw a blob for each club you need three facts: where the middle of the club is, how spread out the kids are, and how many kids are in the club. Fitting means working these out from kids you have already seen.

The middle is the average spot: add up everyone's position and divide by the number of kids. The spread comes from how far kids usually stand from that middle. The club size is a head count.

You can also let all clubs share one shape, or force the shapes to be simple. The next three parts show how.

Fitting means choosing the numbers that describe each class's hill. The standard choice is maximum likelihood: pick the values that make the training data as probable as possible. For Gaussians the answers are simple.

  • The class prior is the fraction of training examples in that class, \hat\pi_k=n_k/n.
  • The center is the average of that class's points.
  • The spread matrix averages (\mathbf{x}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})(\mathbf{x}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})^{\top} over the class's points. It records how much each coordinate varies and how much pairs of coordinates vary together.

Count the numbers to estimate. In two dimensions a full spread matrix has 3 free numbers, and in 10 dimensions it has 55. More freedom lets the model bend to the data, and also lets it chase noise when examples are few. The next sections describe ways to spend fewer numbers.

The joint log-likelihood separates by class, so every block has a closed-form maximizer:

\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\log\pi_{y_i}+\log\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i;\boldsymbol{\mu}_{y_i},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{y_i})\right]
\hat\pi_k=\frac{n_k}{n},\qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_k=\frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i:\,y_i=k}\mathbf{x}_i,\qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_k=\frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i:\,y_i=k}(\mathbf{x}_i-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_k)(\mathbf{x}_i-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_k)^{\top}

The MLE of \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k divides by n_k, so it is biased low; dividing by n_k-1 removes the bias. Neither version is invertible when n_k\le d, since the sample covariance has rank at most \min(n_k-1,d). That is where QDA breaks and constraints or priors become necessary.

Constraining \boldsymbol{\Sigma} trades variance for bias. The next three subsections cover the tied, diagonal and MAP routes, and the cheat sheet at the end lists the full ladder with parameter counts.

1.3.1Tied covariances

Here is a shortcut. Pretend all the clubs have exactly the same shape, an oval with the same size and the same tilt, only standing in different places. Now every kid in every club helps you work out that one shape. More kids means a steadier guess.

The shortcut has a cost. If the clubs really do have different shapes, your one shared shape is wrong for some of them. But when you have only a few kids from each club, the steadier guess often wins.

"Tied" or "pooled" covariance means every class uses the same spread matrix. To estimate it, subtract each class's own mean from its points so that every class is centered at zero, then average the outer products over all training points together:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i:\,y_i=k}(\mathbf{x}_i-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_k)(\mathbf{x}_i-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_k)^{\top}

This is a weighted average of the class spread matrices, with each class counting in proportion to its size. Pooling builds one estimate from all n points instead of K estimates from about n/K points each, so it is less noisy. In return the model can only draw straight boundaries (section 1.2). If the true spreads differ a lot, tying them leaves an error that more data cannot fix.

The part of the log-likelihood that depends on a shared \boldsymbol{\Sigma} is

-\frac{n}{2}\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\sum_{k}\mathbf{S}_k\right),\qquad \mathbf{S}_k=\sum_{i:\,y_i=k}(\mathbf{x}_i-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_k)(\mathbf{x}_i-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_k)^{\top}

This has the same form as a single Gaussian with scatter \sum_k\mathbf{S}_k and n points, so the maximizer is

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k}\mathbf{S}_k=\sum_{k}\frac{n_k}{n}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_k

That is the within-class scatter \mathbf{S}_W divided by n. Dividing by n-K gives the unbiased version. The model has d(d+1)/2 covariance parameters whatever the value of K, which makes LDA workable at moderate d. Its boundaries are linear (section 1.2), and the same \mathbf{S}_W returns as the denominator of Fisher's criterion in section 1.4.

1.3.2Diagonal covariances

Here is another way to keep the guess simple. Let each blob stretch left and right or up and down, but never tilt. Then for each club you only need two facts: how wide it is and how tall it is.

That is much less to work out, so your guess stays steady even with only a few kids. But if the real clubs lean diagonally, the untilted blobs fit badly.

A diagonal covariance matrix has zeros everywhere except the main diagonal:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k=\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma_{k1}^{2},\,\sigma_{k2}^{2},\,\ldots,\,\sigma_{kd}^{2}\right)

It says the measurements vary independently of each other inside a class: once you know the class, a person's height tells you nothing more about their weight. Each measurement then gets its own bell curve, and the class hill is the product of those curves. The ovals stay lined up with the axes. Each variance is estimated on its own as the average squared distance from the class mean, so you need d numbers per class instead of d(d+1)/2.

With different variances per class the boundary is still curved, and with one shared set of variances it is straight. Strong correlation between measurements breaks the independence assumption, and the lab shows how much that costs.

Taking \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{k1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{kd}^{2}) factorizes the class-conditional density:

p(\mathbf{x}\mid y=k)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{N}(x_j;\mu_{kj},\sigma_{kj}^{2})

This is Gaussian naive Bayes: the features are independent given the label. The MLE decouples by coordinate, and the score becomes a sum over features:

\hat\sigma_{kj}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i:\,y_i=k}(x_{ij}-\hat\mu_{kj})^{2},\qquad \delta_k(\mathbf{x})=\log\pi_k-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\log\sigma_{kj}^{2}+\frac{(x_j-\mu_{kj})^{2}}{\sigma_{kj}^{2}}\right]

The structures form a ladder of decreasing flexibility: full \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k, tied \boldsymbol{\Sigma}, diagonal \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k, tied diagonal, spherical \sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}, and the identity. Each step down costs bias and saves variance. Conditional independence is false whenever features correlate within a class, yet the classifier can still do well, because only the ordering of the scores has to be right and not the accuracy of the densities. It does hurt when the correlation changes the boundary, which the lab at this level makes visible.

1.3.3MAP estimation

Imagine you have only seen two kids from a club. You should not be very sure what the club's blob looks like. A smart guesser starts with a sensible hunch, such as "clubs are usually round blobs of medium size", and lets the kids they actually see move the hunch step by step.

With just a few kids you stay close to your hunch. With lots of kids, what you see takes over. That is MAP estimation: a guess that starts from a hunch and learns from the data.

Maximum likelihood looks only at the data. MAP (maximum a posteriori) estimation also uses a prior, a belief about the parameters before any data arrives. It picks the parameters that maximize likelihood times prior.

A good way to read a prior is as imaginary extra data. Suppose your prior guess for a class center is a point \boldsymbol{\mu}_0, and you trust it as much as \kappa_0 real examples. The MAP center is then a weighted average of the data average and the guess:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\text{MAP}}=\frac{n\,\bar{\mathbf{x}}+\kappa_0\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_0}{n+\kappa_0}

With few examples the guess pulls hard, and as n grows the data dominate and the estimate approaches the plain average. Priors on the spread matrix work the same way, and they fix a real problem. With two points in two dimensions, the maximum-likelihood ellipse is a flat line, which is infinitely sure that nothing lies off it. A prior made of some imaginary round blobs keeps the ellipse from collapsing.

\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\argmax_{\theta}\;\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)+\log p(\theta)

For the class probabilities, a Dirichlet prior with parameters \alpha_k gives \hat\pi_k=(n_k+\alpha_k-1)/(n+\sum_j\alpha_j-K), which is Laplace smoothing when every \alpha_k=2. For the Gaussian parameters the conjugate choice is the normal-inverse-Wishart prior, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k/\kappa_0) and \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\sim\mathrm{IW}(\boldsymbol{\Psi},\nu_0). The posterior is again normal-inverse-Wishart, and its joint mode is

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_k=\frac{n_k\bar{\mathbf{x}}_k+\kappa_0\boldsymbol{\mu}_0}{n_k+\kappa_0},\qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_k=\frac{\boldsymbol{\Psi}+\mathbf{S}_k+\frac{\kappa_0 n_k}{\kappa_0+n_k}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_k-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_k-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^{\top}}{\nu_0+n_k+d+2}

Reading the terms: \boldsymbol{\Psi} acts like the scatter of \nu_0 pseudo-observations with covariance \boldsymbol{\Psi}/\nu_0, and the denominator \nu_0+n_k+d+2 replaces the n_k of the MLE. The last term in the numerator inflates the estimate when the class mean sits far from \boldsymbol{\mu}_0. As n_k\to\infty the MAP and ML estimates agree. When n_k\le d the MAP covariance is still positive definite because \boldsymbol{\Psi} is. Taking \boldsymbol{\Psi}=\lambda\mathbf{I} with \kappa_0=\nu_0=0 gives ridge-style shrinkage, (\mathbf{S}_k+\lambda\mathbf{I})/(n_k+d+2), the idea behind regularized discriminant analysis.

The lab uses \boldsymbol{\Psi}=\nu_0 s_0^{2}\mathbf{I}, so s_0 is the radius of the prior blob. In two dimensions the denominator is \nu_0+n+4.

1.4Nearest centroid classifier and Fisher's linear discriminant analysis

Nearest centroid. Find the middle of each club. That middle is called the centroid. A new kid joins the club whose middle is closest, like walking to the nearest campfire. It is very simple, and it works well when all the clubs are round blobs of the same size.

Fisher's flashlight. Imagine the kids are standing in the dark and you shine a flashlight from the side so their shadows land on a long wall. If you pick the angle well, the orange shadows pile up in one spot and the blue shadows pile up in another, so you can tell the clubs apart just by looking at the wall. Fisher's idea is to pick the angle that pulls the two shadow piles as far apart as possible while keeping each pile tight.

Nearest centroid sends each point to the class whose mean is closest in ordinary straight-line distance. It is GDA with the simplest possible hill: round blobs of equal size (\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}) and equal class priors. The boundary is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the means. It works best when the measurements are on the same scale and spread evenly.

Fisher's linear discriminant asks a different question. Onto which line should we project the data so the classes are as well separated as possible? "Separated" means that the two projected means are far apart compared with how spread out each class is along that line:

J(\mathbf{w})=\frac{\left(\text{distance between projected means}\right)^{2}}{\text{total spread of the classes along the line}}

The best direction is \mathbf{w}\propto\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_W^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_0). When the blobs are round, this is the same direction as the line between the means. When they are tilted, the best direction turns away from the long axis of the spread so that the shadows overlap less. A threshold along this line is a classifier, and it is the same line that GDA with a shared spread matrix uses (section 1.2).

The nearest-centroid rule is

\hat y=\argmin_{k}\,\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_k\|^{2}

which is GDA with \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I} and equal priors, since then \delta_k=-\|\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k\|^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})+\log\pi_k. Unequal priors add an offset 2\sigma^{2}\log\pi_k to the squared distance comparison. Replacing the Euclidean distance with the Mahalanobis distance of the pooled covariance gives LDA with equal priors. Equivalently, LDA is nearest centroid after whitening, \mathbf{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}, up to the prior offset.

Fisher's criterion for a projection direction \mathbf{w} is the generalized Rayleigh quotient

J(\mathbf{w})=\frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_B\,\mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_W\,\mathbf{w}},\qquad \mathbf{S}_B=\sum_{k}n_k(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_k-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_k-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})^{\top},\qquad \mathbf{S}_W=\sum_{k}\mathbf{S}_k

Maximizers solve the generalized eigenproblem \mathbf{S}_B\mathbf{w}=\lambda\,\mathbf{S}_W\mathbf{w}, so the directions are the top eigenvectors of \mathbf{S}_W^{-1}\mathbf{S}_B. The between-class scatter \mathbf{S}_B has rank at most K-1, so there are at most K-1 useful directions, and Fisher's method is a supervised dimension reduction to at most K-1 dimensions. For two classes \mathbf{S}_B\propto(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_1-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_0)(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_1-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_0)^{\top}, and the solution is

\mathbf{w}^{*}\propto\mathbf{S}_W^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_1-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_0),\qquad J(\mathbf{w}^{*})\propto(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_1-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_0)^{\top}\mathbf{S}_W^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_1-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_0)

This is the same direction as the shared-covariance GDA weight vector in section 1.2, but the two derivations assume different things. Fisher uses only means and within-class scatter, makes no Gaussian assumption, and says nothing about where to put the threshold. GDA assumes Gaussian classes with a common covariance, which fixes both the threshold and the effect of the priors. With K classes, the LDA decision rule lives in the (K-1)-dimensional affine span of the class means, so projecting onto all Fisher directions and running nearest centroid there reproduces it, up to the prior offsets.

Cheat sheet and quiz

Every model on this page is the same classifier with a different constraint on the covariance. Here K is the number of classes and d the number of features.

Fitting recipes, with \mathbf{S}_k the class scatter matrix:

  • \hat\pi_k=n_k/n and \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_k=\bar{\mathbf{x}}_k for every model above.
  • Full: \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_k=\mathbf{S}_k/n_k. Tied: \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\sum_k\mathbf{S}_k/n. Diagonal: keep only the diagonal of those matrices.
  • MAP: add pseudo-counts as in section 1.3.3 and divide by \nu_0+n_k+d+2.
  • Fisher, two classes: \mathbf{w}\propto\mathbf{S}_W^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_1-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_0).
