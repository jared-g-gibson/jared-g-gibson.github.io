Part 1 · What is it?

The naive Bayes classifier

Pretend you are a detective deciding whether a letter is junk mail. You look for clues. Does it say FREE? Does it say WINNER? Does it mention homework? Junk mail says FREE a lot, and a letter from your teacher almost never does. So every clue gives a little push toward JUNK or toward NOT JUNK. You add up all the pushes, and the side with the bigger pile wins.

It is called naive because the detective acts as if every clue works alone. Real clues sometimes team up (FREE and WINNER often show up together), but pretending they don't makes the detective fast, and surprisingly good.

Naive Bayes starts from Bayes' rule: P(spam | words) = P(words | spam) × P(spam) ÷ P(words). Computing P(words | spam) exactly would need data on every possible combination of words, which is far too many combinations to ever see.

The naive assumption fixes this: once you know the class, each word shows up independently of the others. Then P(words | spam) is just a product, P(free | spam) × P(winner | spam) × …. To classify, compute that product for each class, multiply by the class's prior, and pick the larger result.

Example with a single clue: 40% of email is spam, and "free" appears in 45% of spam but only 5% of normal email. The odds are 0.40 × 0.45 : 0.60 × 0.05 = 0.18 : 0.03 = 6 : 1, so the email is about 86% likely to be spam.

Naive Bayes is a generative classifier: it models the joint p(x, y) = p(y) p(x | y) and classifies with Bayes' rule. For y ∈ {1…C} and x ∈ ℝD, the "naive" assumption is class-conditional independence, p(x | y=c, θ) = ∏j=1..D p(xj | y=c, θjc). As a graphical model, y is the only parent of every xj.

This is a statistical-efficiency trade. A full joint over D binary features needs O(C·2D) parameters; the factored form needs O(CD). The decision rule is ŷ = argmaxc [ log πc + Σj log p(xj | θjc) ]. The assumption is rarely true, yet the classifier is often accurate anyway: the argmax can be right while the posterior probability is badly miscalibrated, because correlated features get counted more than once.

p(y=c | x) = π_c · ∏ⱼ p(xⱼ | θ_jc) ───────────────────────────── Σ_c′ π_c′ · ∏ⱼ p(xⱼ | θ_jc′)

Part 1 · Example models

Choosing a likelihood: Bernoulli, multinomial, Gaussian

Different clues need different scoreboards. A yes/no clue ("does it have wings?") uses a yes/no scoreboard. A counting clue ("how many times does the word FREE show up?") uses a tally scoreboard. A measuring clue ("how tall is it?") uses a measuring scoreboard, where most animals of one kind are close to the same height and very few are far away from it.

Which version of naive Bayes you use depends on what your features look like:

  • Bernoulli: each feature is yes/no (is the word in the email?). For every class and feature you store one number, the chance θ that the answer is "yes".
  • Multinomial: each feature is a count (how many times does each word appear?). For every class you store the share of that class's words that each vocabulary word makes up.
  • Gaussian: each feature is a measurement (height, blood pressure). For every class and feature you store a mean and a spread, which describes a bell curve.

Different features can even use different kinds of model, because each feature is handled on its own.

The factorization lets each feature pick its own class-conditional family:

  • Bernoulli: p(x | y=c) = ∏j θjcxj (1−θjc)1−xj for binary features.
  • Multinomial: p(x | y=c) = [n! / ∏j xj!] ∏j θjcxj with Σj θjc = 1. The multinomial coefficient does not depend on c, so it cancels in the posterior.
  • Gaussian: p(xj | y=c) = N(xj; μjc, σ²jc). A categorical feature uses Cat(θjc).

The log-posterior is linear in the features for Bernoulli and multinomial NB, and for Gaussian NB with class-independent variances. With class-specific variances the boundary is quadratic. That is the first hint of the logistic-regression link in a later section.

Part 1 · Model fitting

Fitting naive Bayes: training is counting

How does the detective learn the clues? By counting! Take 100 old letters where you already know the answer. If 30 were junk and the word FREE was in 12 of them, then FREE shows up in 12 out of 30 junk letters. That is the whole lesson, one count per clue.

There is one trap. If FREE never appeared in a junk letter you looked at, the detective would shout "FREE can NEVER be in junk!" and stop listening to every other clue. The fix is to pretend you saw every clue a tiny bit, like adding one pretend letter. Then nothing is ever "impossible".

"Fitting" means choosing the probabilities from training data. The best-fitting values (the maximum likelihood estimates) turn out to be plain relative frequencies: the class prior is the fraction of training emails in that class, and the chance of a word given a class is the fraction of that class's emails containing the word. For a measurement feature, you use each class's sample mean and sample variance. One pass through the data is enough, with no trial and error.

The problem is zero counts. If "prize" never appeared in a spam email, the estimate is P(prize | spam) = 0, and then any email containing "prize" gets probability 0 of being spam no matter what else it says. Laplace (add-one) smoothing adds one pretend example of each outcome: (count + 1) ÷ (total + 2) for a yes/no feature.

With i.i.d. data, the log-likelihood decomposes over parameters: log p(D | θ) = Σc Nc log πc + Σj Σc Σi: yi=c log p(xij | θjc). Each term is maximized separately. The prior comes from a Lagrange multiplier for Σ πc = 1, and the per-feature terms are one-dimensional MLEs. Everything is closed form and costs O(ND) to compute.

The MLE fails when a count is zero: the log-likelihood contribution is unbounded below, and one zero factor wipes out the whole product at prediction time. Adding pseudo-counts α is the standard fix, and the next section shows it is exactly Bayesian inference under a Beta or Dirichlet prior. The contrast to remember: here the fit is a counting formula, whereas logistic regression has no closed form and needs iterative optimization (Newton, IRLS, or bound optimization).

π̂_c = N_c / N θ̂_jc = N_jc / N_c Bernoulli: examples of class c with feature j on θ̂_jc = N_jc / Σ_j′ N_j′c Multinomial: word j's share of class c's tokens μ̂_jc = mean of xⱼ in class c; σ̂²_jc = variance of xⱼ in class c

Part 1 · Bayesian naive Bayes

Bayesian naive Bayes: start with a guess, let data change it

Before you look at any letters, you may already have a hunch, like "FREE is probably a bit suspicious". Bayesian naive Bayes lets you write down that hunch first, and then change your mind as you see real letters. With only a few letters you mostly keep your hunch. With lots of letters, the letters win.

Instead of locking in one number for "chance of prize in spam", keep a whole range of believable values and update it with data. The starting range is a prior. A convenient choice is the Beta distribution with two numbers a and b, which act like imaginary emails: a imaginary spam emails with the word and b without it.

After seeing real data (k of n spam emails had the word), your updated best guess is (k + a) ÷ (n + a + b). Few real emails: the imaginary ones matter a lot. Many real emails: they barely matter. Laplace smoothing is the special case a = b = 1, a prior that treats every value as equally likely.

Treat the parameters as random: π ~ Dir(α) and, independently, θjc ~ Beta(a, b) for Bernoulli features (Dirichlet for categorical or multinomial). Because the likelihood and the prior factorize, so does the posterior: θjc | D ~ Beta(a + Njc, b + Nc − Njc), with the same closed-form update for every parameter.

For prediction, integrate the parameters out instead of plugging in a point estimate. Under the independent posteriors, the posterior predictive also factorizes, and for a Bernoulli feature it equals the posterior mean: p(xj=1 | y=c, D) = (Njc + a) / (Nc + a + b). With a = b = 1 this is add-one smoothing (Laplace's rule of succession). The MAP estimate differs, (Njc + a − 1) / (Nc + a + b − 2), and with a = b = 1 it reduces to the MLE. So "Laplace smoothing" is a posterior mean, not a MAP. The Beta-Binomial marginal likelihood is also closed form, which allows model comparison.

prior θ ~ Beta(a, b) posterior θ | D ~ Beta(a + k, b + n − k) (k successes in n trials) mean (k + a) / (n + a + b) MAP (k + a − 1) / (n + a + b − 2)

Part 1 · The connection

Naive Bayes and logistic regression

Both helpers keep score by adding up clues, but they get their clue strengths in different ways. Naive Bayes counts: "this clue is 6 times more common in junk". Logistic regression practices: it tries strengths, checks how often it guesses right, and keeps adjusting.

Here is the funny difference. If you tell naive Bayes the same clue three times, it counts the clue three times and gets overexcited. Logistic regression notices the clue repeated and gives each copy a smaller say.

Both methods end with a score that adds up the clues, then squash that score into a probability with the S-shaped sigmoid curve. For naive Bayes with yes/no clues, the log-odds of spam is the prior log-odds plus one term per clue. That is exactly the shape of logistic regression: log-odds = w₀ + w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + ….

So the two methods can produce the same kind of answer. The difference is where the weights come from. Naive Bayes reads each weight off the counts, one clue at a time. Logistic regression searches for the weights that make its predicted probabilities fit the training labels best, all clues at once. If clues overlap, naive Bayes double-counts them, and logistic regression can correct for that.

For binary y and Bernoulli features, the NB log-odds is affine in x: log p(y=1|x)/p(y=0|x) = w₀ + wᵀx with wj = log[θj1(1−θj0) / (θj0(1−θj1))] and w₀ = log(π₁/π₀) + Σj log[(1−θj1)/(1−θj0)]. Hence p(y=1|x) = σ(w₀ + wᵀx). Gaussian NB with shared variances gives wj = (μj1 − μj0)/σj².

So NB is a point inside logistic regression's hypothesis class, and the two differ only in the estimator. NB maximizes the joint likelihood Σ log p(xi, yi) (closed form). LR maximizes the conditional likelihood Σ log p(yi | xi), which is convex but has no closed form. When the NB assumptions hold and n → ∞, the two agree. When features are correlated, NB's joint-likelihood fit double-counts evidence, which produces overconfident posteriors, and LR's conditional fit can reduce the weights to compensate. Gaussian NB with unequal class variances has a quadratic boundary, so it escapes the LR class.

NB log-odds = log(π₁/π₀) + Σⱼ [ xⱼ·log(θⱼ₁/θⱼ₀) + (1−xⱼ)·log((1−θⱼ₁)/(1−θⱼ₀)) ] = w₀ + Σⱼ wⱼ xⱼ → p(y=1 | x) = σ(w₀ + wᵀx)

Part 2 · Two ways to build a classifier

Generative vs. discriminative classifiers

Generative means "learn to draw." You learn what cats look like and what dogs look like, well enough to sketch a new one. To decide what a mystery animal is, you ask, "does it look more like my cat drawings or my dog drawings?"

Discriminative means "learn the fence." You do not learn to draw anything. You only learn where to put a fence between cats and dogs. You can't draw a cat, but you are very good at telling which side of the fence an animal is on.

Drawing is more work, but it helps when you only have a few examples, or when some clues are missing. A fence is less work and is usually better once you have seen lots of animals.

A generative classifier models how the data are produced in each class, P(x | class) and P(class), then uses Bayes' rule to get P(class | x). Naive Bayes is the standard example. A discriminative classifier models P(class | x) directly, without caring how x itself is distributed. Logistic regression is the standard example.

Rules of thumb: generative models tend to win when training data are scarce, when the data are partly unlabeled or have missing values, or when you want to simulate new data. Discriminative models tend to win when you have plenty of labeled data, because they spend all their effort on the boundary and make fewer assumptions.

Generative classifiers fit p(x, y | θ) by maximizing the joint likelihood; discriminative ones fit p(y | x, θ) by maximizing the conditional likelihood. Ng & Jordan (2001) compared NB and LR directly. The discriminative model has the lower asymptotic error, since it does not depend on the independence assumption being true. The generative model may reach its (higher) asymptote much faster: roughly O(log D) training examples versus O(D) for LR. That gives two regimes: NB can win with little data, and LR wins once n is large enough.

Beyond that, the generative joint gives you p(x) for outlier detection and for semi-supervised learning with EM, plus principled handling of missing features by marginalizing them out. The discriminative model gives better-calibrated probabilities, accepts arbitrary correlated and overlapping features without modeling them, and is more robust to misspecification of p(x | y).

Bridge · Bound optimization

Where bound optimization comes in

Imagine you are in a bumpy park in thick fog, trying to reach the lowest spot. You can't see far, so you do a trick. You hold a big smooth bowl over the bumpy ground, so the bowl touches the ground right under your feet and is never lower than the ground. Sliding to the bottom of the bowl is easy, and wherever you land, the real ground under you is at least that low. Then you do it again from the new spot. Each round you go down, and you never go up.

Some functions are hard to minimize directly, like the "mistake score" of logistic regression. Bound optimization (also called MM, "majorize-minimize") replaces the hard function with an easy one, such as a parabola, that lies on or above it and touches it at your current guess. You jump to the bottom of the parabola. Because the parabola is above the real curve, the real function is now at least as low as the parabola's bottom, which is at most as high as where you started. Repeat until you stop improving.

To minimize L(θ), construct a surrogate Q(θ | θt) with Q(θ | θt) ≥ L(θ) for all θ and Q(θt | θt) = L(θt). Set θt+1 = argmin Q(· | θt). Then L(θt+1) ≤ Q(θt+1 | θt) ≤ Q(θt | θt) = L(θt), so descent is monotone by construction, with no line search. (For maximization, use a lower bound.)

EM is bound optimization. For a latent-variable log-likelihood, Jensen's inequality gives ℓ(θ) ≥ Σi Σz qi(z) log[p(xi, z | θ) / qi(z)], tight when qi = p(z | xi, θt). For naive Bayes with unlabeled data, the E-step computes NB posteriors over the missing labels, and the M-step is the same counting formula as before but with fractional (soft) counts. This is how generative classifiers use unlabeled data.

Logistic regression. The Hessian of the negative log-likelihood satisfies H(w) ⪯ ¼ XᵀX (since σ(1−σ) ≤ ¼), so the quadratic with fixed curvature B = ¼ XᵀX is a global upper bound (Böhning & Lindsay, 1988). The update w ← w − B⁻¹ ∇L(w) reuses one matrix that can be factored once. It is slower per iteration than Newton's method but guaranteed monotone, while Newton's local quadratic is not a global bound and can overshoot.
