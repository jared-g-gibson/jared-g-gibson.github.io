One equation, every shape of joint uncertainty

The multivariate Gaussian is the rare distribution where slicing it, averaging over part of it, or adding it to another copy of itself never produces anything but more Gaussians. Adjust the controls below and watch why that holds.

The density function

$$ \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma) \;=\; \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D/2}\,|\boldsymbol\Sigma|^{1/2}} \, \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)\right) $$
$D$
the dimension — how many correlated variables sit in the vector $\mathbf{y}$.
$\boldsymbol\mu$
the mean vector, one entry per dimension — where the distribution is centered.
$\boldsymbol\Sigma$
the covariance matrix — variances on the diagonal, covariances off it.
$|\boldsymbol\Sigma|$
the determinant of $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ — the "volume" the distribution occupies, and exactly what the normalizer needs to keep the density integrating to 1.
$\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}$
the precision matrix — its off-diagonal entries reveal which variables stay dependent once every other variable is already known.
$(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)$
the squared Mahalanobis distance — a z-score that accounts for correlation. Every point at the same value sits on the same elliptical shell.

Properties worth knowing cold

Closed under marginalization

Integrate any subset of variables out and what's left is still Gaussian, with mean and covariance read directly off the original $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ — no integral to actually solve.

Closed under conditioning

Fix any subset of variables to observed values and the distribution over the rest is Gaussian again, with a shifted mean and a covariance that can only shrink.

Closed under affine transformation

If $\mathbf{y}$ is Gaussian, so is $A\mathbf{y}+b$ for any matrix $A$ and vector $b$. Linear combinations and projections of a Gaussian stay Gaussian.

Uncorrelated means independent

For jointly Gaussian variables, zero covariance implies independence — a shortcut that fails for almost every other family of distributions.

Fully described by two moments

The mean vector and covariance matrix are the whole story. No skew, no kurtosis, no extra shape parameters to fit.

Elliptical contours

Every level set of the density is an ellipsoid centered at $\mu$, with axes along the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ and lengths set by the square roots of its eigenvalues.

Maximum entropy for its moments

Among every distribution with a given mean and covariance, the Gaussian is the one that assumes the least beyond those two facts.

Sums stay Gaussian

Add two independent Gaussian vectors, or take any linear combination of jointly Gaussian variables, and the result is Gaussian, with variances and covariances simply adding.

Conditioning vs. marginalizing

Split $\mathbf{y}$ into two blocks, $\mathbf{y}_a$ and $\mathbf{y}_b$, and partition $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ to match: $\boldsymbol\mu=(\boldsymbol\mu_a,\boldsymbol\mu_b)$ and $\boldsymbol\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{aa} & \boldsymbol\Sigma_{ab}\\ \boldsymbol\Sigma_{ba} & \boldsymbol\Sigma_{bb}\end{pmatrix}$. Marginalizing asks what $y_a$ looks like on its own, averaged over every possible $y_b$. Conditioning asks what $y_a$ looks like once $y_b$ is pinned to one observed value. Different questions, both with Gaussian answers.

Marginalizing out $y_b$

$$ p(\mathbf{y}_a) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}_a \mid \boldsymbol\mu_a,\; \boldsymbol\Sigma_{aa}) $$

To marginalize, delete the rows and columns belonging to the other variables. That's it — no computation beyond bookkeeping, because the sub-vector and sub-matrix you keep already are the marginal's mean and covariance.

Conditioning on $y_b$

$$ p(\mathbf{y}_a \mid \mathbf{y}_b) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{y}_a \mid \boldsymbol\mu_a+\boldsymbol\Sigma_{ab}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}_b-\boldsymbol\mu_b),\;\; \boldsymbol\Sigma_{aa}-\boldsymbol\Sigma_{ab}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{ba}\big) $$

The mean shifts away from $\mu_a$ in proportion to how far $y_b$ landed from its own mean, scaled by how strongly the blocks covary. The covariance term, $\boldsymbol\Sigma_{ab}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{ba}$, is always positive semi-definite, which is why observing $y_b$ never increases uncertainty about $y_a$ — it can only hold it steady or shrink it.

One more piece worth keeping: the inverse of $\boldsymbol\Sigma$, the precision matrix $\boldsymbol\Lambda=\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}$, has the same block structure. Its $\boldsymbol\Lambda_{aa}$ block turns out to be exactly the inverse of the conditional covariance above — the identity that makes Gaussian graphical models work. A zero entry in $\boldsymbol\Lambda$ means those two variables are independent once everything else is known, which is a stronger statement than an entry of $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ being zero, which only rules out marginal correlation.
